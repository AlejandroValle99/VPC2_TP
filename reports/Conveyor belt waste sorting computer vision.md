# Architecting real-time recyclable sorting beyond YOLO

A basic YOLO detector is necessary but nowhere near sufficient: the deployed and research literature converges on a layered stack — a nano-scale YOLO or RT-DETR detector for real-time localization, a ByteTrack- or BoT-SORT-style tracker for persistent identity as items cross the belt, optional SAM2-based mask refinement for actuator targeting, and open-vocabulary detectors (YOLO-World, Grounding DINO) used only as an offline pseudo-labeling tool, since they trail fine-tuned closed-set detectors by roughly 3–7× in mAP on real waste imagery. Commercial systems from AMP Robotics and Recycleye report light-duty pick rates of 55–140 picks per minute, while ZenRobotics/Terex's Heavy Picker — built for objects up to 40 kg, a different and not directly comparable weight class — runs a slower ≈38 picks/minute; purity claims reach 99% at two vendor sources, but a separate, vendor-authored IEEE Spectrum estimate puts realistic purity for "current systems" at a more conservative 80–95%. Every one of these figures is vendor-self-published or vendor-authored trade press, never independently audited, so they read as aspirational targets rather than ground truth. For the required peer-reviewed "real case study no older than 7 years," Koskinopoulou et al. (IEEE Robotics and Automation Magazine, 2021) is the strongest available anchor — its abstract confirms deployment and assessment at a real industrial waste plant — but its full text was unreachable across three independent attempts in this research, so no number beyond that bare deployment fact should be cited from it; Recycleye's re3 Reading MRF case (letsrecycle.com, June 9, 2022) is the strongest dated, numbers-backed companion citation. RGB cameras are the dominant sensing choice for arm-based robotic picking industry-wide, positioned in the literature as complementary to, not a replacement for, NIR/hyperspectral spectroscopy, which remains the incumbent for the chemical/resin-level identification RGB cannot see. Two design opportunities stand out as genuinely open: a market-price-weighted sorting throughput metric — searched for directly across robotics, reinforcement-learning, circular-economy, and queueing-theory literature and not found at the per-decision level — and a SAM-based single-object-to-cluttered-scene compositing pipeline, whose building blocks all exist separately but have never been assembled and tested on recyclables. Monocular depth estimation (UniDepth, Depth Anything V2, Depth Pro) is mature enough for volume proxying but unapplied to waste sorting specifically, and vision-only mass estimation without a physical scale remains unvalidated anywhere in the literature found. Recyclable commodity prices are volatile enough — PET fell by roughly two-thirds and natural HDPE by about a third within 2025 alone, and mixed glass carries a negative price — that any value-weighted design must treat price as a live, sometimes-negative input rather than a fixed positive constant.

## RGB vision complements, rather than replaces, NIR/hyperspectral sorting

The directly-verified literature draws a consistent line between two generations of sorting technology. Traditional automated optical sorters — long established, pre-dating 2019 — rely on near-infrared and hyperspectral spectroscopy to identify material or resin type by chemical signature and eject items with air jets; the AMP Robotics IEEE Spectrum piece treats hyperspectral imaging as this existing, separate incumbent technology, distinct from the RGB "computer vision and image analysis" approach AMP itself uses ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-guided-robots-are-ready-to-sort-your-recyclables)). The newer generation of AI robotic pickers profiled above — AMP, Recycleye, ZenRobotics, Machinex — and Greyparrot's analytics units are primarily RGB-camera-plus-deep-learning systems that identify objects visually by shape, color, texture, and printed branding; Greyparrot's Analyzer specifically uses an industrial 20×-optical-zoom RGB camera (The Imaging Source DFK 39GX265-Z20), per a source seen consistently across multiple search snippets though not directly fetched ([Imaging Source, Oct 2024](https://www.theimagingsource.com/en-us/resource/news/2024/10/02/)).

Specim's directly-fetched technical comparison is explicit about why the two modalities are not substitutes: RGB cameras provide data "similar to human vision" but are limited to three broad color bands, so "identification capability is minimal" for material sorting, whereas hyperspectral imaging records hundreds of spectral channels — including non-visible bands — capturing a chemical "signature" unique to each material; the piece illustrates this with a 930 nm spectral feature tied to nut oil in an almond-sorting case that an RGB camera "missed... completely" ([Specim: Hyperspectral Technology vs. RGB](https://www.specim.com/hyperspectral-technology-vs-rgb/)). This is precisely the limitation that motivates SpectralWaste, a directly-fetched arXiv preprint built from a prototype conveyor belt on the actual separation line of an **operational plastic waste sorting facility** — a genuine real deployment, not a lab mockup. Its dataset pairs RGB imagery (1200×1184, 8-bit) with hyperspectral cubes (224 contiguous bands, 900–1700 nm, 600×640×224, 16-bit); a CMX architecture fusing both modalities reached 58.2% mIoU, its best reported segmentation result, and a label-transfer method improved RGB-to-hyperspectral annotation alignment by 23.3 points (79.6% vs. 56.3% baseline), with processing throughput of 54.7–157.1 images/second depending on architecture ([arXiv 2403.18033](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.18033v1), peer-review status unconfirmed).

The practical implication for a student team building an RGB-only pipeline is that this choice is squarely consistent with the *current dominant approach for robotic (arm-based) picking* — none of AMP, Recycleye, ZenRobotics, or Machinex is confirmed to use hyperspectral sensing in their production systems — but it inherits a literature-acknowledged limitation on visually-similar, chemically-different materials (PET vs. PVC bottles of similar appearance being the classic example), which is a legitimate, citable limitation to name explicitly in a paper's future-work section rather than something to paper over. No source in this research gave a confirmed sensor bill-of-materials for AMP Neuron, ZenRobotics, or Machinex SamurAI's vision systems beyond generic "vision system"/"AI" language, and no 2019+ dated source for X-ray or LIBS (laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy) sorting — both known industrially for metals/alloys — was located in this pass.

## A four-stage inference pipeline beats a lone detector on every axis

The published numbers on detectors, trackers, SAM2, and open-vocabulary models — assembled below into one table — make a consistent case: no single model does everything, and the winning architecture chains a fast closed-set detector, a lightweight tracker, optional mask refinement, and an open-vocabulary fallback into a single pipeline rather than relying on any one component alone.

That paper reports ZeroWaste-f as 4,503 labeled images and 27,000 annotated instances across 4 classes, with cardboard exceeding 66% of annotations and metal under 2% — a different count than the original ZeroWaste dataset paper's own figures, discussed and reconciled in the training-data section below.)*

**Detector choice.** Waste-detection mAP varies enormously by dataset and domain — from 58% on domestic-waste imagery to 94.6% on desert/drone imagery to a 34–52 mAP band on the real, cluttered ZeroWaste-f benchmark — so no single headline number should be transplanted uncritically into a design decision; the team should validate against its own material stream and camera conditions. That said, nano/small YOLO variants consistently land in the 15–40 ms per-frame range on GPU hardware from RTX 3090 to Tesla T4 to Jetson-class edge devices, comfortably inside typical belt-sorting cycle budgets, while the larger transformer-based closed-set detectors (DINO, Co-DETR, DETA) trade 15–20 mAP points of accuracy for latency figures no source reported — making them better suited to offline labeling/benchmarking than in-line actuator control. RT-DETR variants show competitive or superior mAP to same-scale YOLO models on waste data (RT-DETR(L) 35.1 vs. YOLO11(L) 34.0 on ZeroWaste-f; RTDRNet-lite's headline 97% mAP@50), making the family a credible YOLO alternative, but its real-time-latency evidence base on waste specifically is thin — RTDRNet-lite's own FPS/latency figures could not be retrieved behind a paywall and CAPTCHA in this research.

**Tracking.** ByteTrack is the tracker most consistently paired with YOLO in practitioner belt-counting writeups, explicitly because a single-camera, single-pass conveyor is closer to "count and forget" tracking than the long-horizon, multi-camera re-identification DeepSORT's appearance-embedding step was built for ([Roboflow: Counting Objects on a Conveyor Belt](https://blog.roboflow.com/counting-objects-conveyor-belt/)). BoT-SORT-ReID edges out ByteTrack on both MOT17 and MOT20 across MOTA/IDF1/HOTA, and its architectural changes — estimating object width/height directly instead of aspect ratio, plus camera-motion compensation — are explicitly framed as suited to "crowded scenes, moving cameras, and warehouse environments where occlusion is constant" ([BoT-SORT explainer, Labellerr](https://www.labellerr.com/blog/bot-sort-tracking/)), which sounds like a belt with partial occlusion and vibration. But this is a **plausible, unverified transfer**: no MOTA/IDF1/HOTA number for any belt, waste-sorting, or industrial-parts dataset exists anywhere in the literature searched — every comparative number above comes from general pedestrian benchmarks. The closest primary source to "ByteTrack on a literal dense conveyor belt" is a peer-reviewed apple-inspection paper combining an orchard-trained YOLOv8, ByteTrack, and a ResNet18 defect classifier with "track-level aggregation... to enforce temporal consistency and reduce prediction oscillation across frames" ([arXiv 2602.19278](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19278)) — its full text and PDF were both directly fetched, but no numeric mAP, FPS, or MOTA/IDF1 figure could be extracted from the retrieved content, leaving this the strongest qualitative precedent without a quantitative one.

**SAM2 and pixel-accurate masks.** No evidence was found of SAM2 applied to conveyor-belt or waste-sorting video anywhere in the literature searched — this is a genuine open gap, not a solved-and-abandoned problem. SAM2's per-variant speeds on an A100 (30–47 FPS) are in the same rough range as YOLO-plus-tracker pipelines, suggesting it is technically fast enough to consider if pixel-accurate masks matter for the downstream actuator — precise air-jet timing or gripper contact-point selection — but A100-class hardware is a far heavier compute footprint than the edge GPUs (Jetson, RTX 3090, T4) seen throughout the rest of this table, a real cost consideration for a student budget. A plausible, currently unvalidated architecture is a fast closed-set detector for triggering and localization, with SAM2 invoked only on candidate regions for mask refinement rather than run densely every frame — but no published work has tested this for belt sorting, so any claim that "SAM2 works well here" would currently be unsupported.

**Open-vocabulary detectors.** This is the best-evidenced sub-question in this research, and the evidence argues clearly against using YOLO-World, Grounding DINO, or OWLv2 as the primary real-time detector. Zero-shot, bare class-name prompts score 6.5–7.3 mAP on ZeroWaste-f — 3–7× below the 34–52 mAP fine-tuned closed-set detectors achieve on the same benchmark — and structured, context-rich prompts roughly double that (12.4–13.5 mAP) without closing the gap. The most useful role found for these models is as a **bootstrapping tool**: Grounding DINO fine-tuned on soft-ensemble pseudo-labels generated by the open-vocabulary models themselves reached 54.3 mAP, beating every closed-set baseline including the best fully-supervised result (51.6 mAP, Co-DETR/DETA). A finding directly actionable for a belt deployment: background regions containing paper or **conveyor-belt texture** resembling cardboard are mislabeled as cardboard at a 60–83% rate by both OWLv2 and YOLO-World, and metal is the weakest class across every model tested (over 50% misclassification, attributed to deformation and reflective surfaces). No FPS or latency figure for any open-vocabulary model on waste data exists in the literature searched. The actionable takeaway: use open-vocabulary detectors offline, for pseudo-labeling rare/novel classes to bootstrap a closed-set detector, or as a secondary human-review flag for genuinely novel materials — not as the actuator-triggering detector.

**Closing the loop.** No paper was found that publishes a formal, end-to-end latency budget — detector plus tracker plus actuator — derived from belt speed and camera field of view, for waste sorting or any adjacent domain; this is a clear, explicit gap. Component numbers exist and can be assembled into a working estimate, though no source performs this calculation itself. Practitioner vision-sizing guidance (general part-counting, not waste-specific) recommends planning for 15–30 fps with 10–20% frame overlap even when the calculated minimum for a given belt speed is much lower, to avoid parts falling between frames under variable belt speed ([Industrial Monitor Direct](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/selecting-a-vision-system-for-random-part-counting-on-conveyors)). Pneumatic ejector valves — the standard rejection mechanism in optical sorting — take roughly 6.5 ms to open and 7.5 ms to close in current hardware, with next-generation valves targeting 3.5/4.5 ms, per a patent document not independently fetched in full ([US Patent 8082838](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8082838)). The one directly relevant, verified end-to-end figure is a CNN classification-plus-robotic-arm system reporting 70 ms mean inference latency at 94.5% classification accuracy and 95.5% robotic sorting success ([Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-23547-3_13)). Stitching these together — nano-detector inference (10–40 ms) plus unquantified tracker overhead plus roughly 4–8 ms actuator response — suggests a real-time budget is very likely achievable on GPU-class edge hardware, but this is this report's own inference bridging separately-sourced figures, not a documented measurement of a full detect-track-actuate loop, and should be presented to the team as a budget to validate experimentally rather than a citable result. Additional throughput figures for commercial systems circulate in secondary industry coverage — ranging from single digits to several hundred items per minute across different system classes — but could not be traced to specific, verifiable primary sources in this research and are not reported here as confirmed figures; the team should apply the same skepticism to any headline throughput number it encounters in vendor or blog coverage until it locates the primary source itself.

## Depth estimation proxies volume convincingly; mass and waste validation are unproven

Four zero-shot metric monocular depth models are active as of 2024–2025 — ZoeDepth, UniDepth (V1/V2), Depth Anything V2 (metric variant), and Apple's Depth Pro — and a cross-paper comparison table from the Depth Pro paper shows no model dominates every domain:

| Dataset (δ1, higher = better) | Depth Pro | Depth Anything V2 | ZoeDepth | UniDepth | Metric3D v2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sun-RGBD (indoor) | 89.0 | 72.4 | 85.7 | **95.8** | 75.6 |
| ETH3D | 41.5 | 36.3 | 34.2 | 25.3 | **87.7** |
| nuScenes (driving) | 49.1 | 17.7 | 28.1 | 83.6 | **82.6** |
| Avg. rank (1–6, lower = better) | **2.5** | 5.8 | 5.3 | 4.2 | 3.7 |

*([Depth Pro, arXiv 2410.02073](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02073))*

For a fixed, roughly-overhead conveyor camera — indoor, controlled lighting, known working distance — the most relevant precedent is UniDepth's indoor performance: δ1 = 98.4%, AbsRel = 5.78% on NYU-Depth v2, ahead of ZoeDepth (95.2%/7.70%) and Metric3D (92.6%/9.38%) ([UniDepth, arXiv 2403.18913](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.18913v1)), since NYU is an indoor, close/mid-range benchmark closer in character to a conveyor rig than nuScenes or ETH3D's driving-style outdoor scenes. Depth Pro itself produces a 2.25-megapixel metric depth map in 0.3 s on a V100 GPU (341.3 ms at HD resolution) from a 504M-parameter model, and its standout advantage is not raw metric accuracy but sharp object-boundary tracing (F1 = 0.409 on Sintel, versus 0.321 for Metric3D v2 and 0.228 for Depth Anything V2) ([arXiv 2410.02073](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02073)) — boundary sharpness plausibly matters more than sub-centimeter absolute depth error for volume-from-height proxying, though this specific tradeoff was not directly tested anywhere. Depth Anything V2 claims over 10× faster inference than diffusion-based approaches like Marigold and offers six metric checkpoints across three scales; its own ViT-L KITTI/ScanNet AbsRel figures (≈0.074 / ≈0.135) come from a secondary aggregator rather than the paper's own results table, which did not surface during direct fetch, so should be treated with correspondingly lower confidence ([Emergent Mind summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/depth-anything-v2-metric-outdoor); primary paper [arXiv 2406.09414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09414)). Critically, **none of these four models has been benchmarked on a conveyor-belt, bin-picking, or produce-grading dataset specifically** — every accuracy figure above transfers from general-purpose indoor/outdoor/driving benchmarks, so a team using any of them for object-height/volume proxying should plan to calibrate against its own rig's ground truth rather than trust these published numbers directly.

Depth-plus-RGB fusion for volume or mass estimation is well established in **agricultural grading**, and the two clearest examples bracket a wide accuracy range depending on object regularity. For oranges — near-ellipsoidal, regular geometry — an RGB-*only* pipeline (no depth camera at all) using a consumer camera, a 30×30 mm calibrated physical marker, and 25 engineered geometric features feeding a stacked regressor reached **R² = 0.971, MAE = 10.22 mL** on 150 fruits, running at 15–25 fps (40–67 ms/fruit), compatible with industrial conveyor speeds; mass came from a physical load cell integrated into the conveyor, not from vision ([PMC12565508](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12565508/)). For garlic cloves — irregular, non-axially-symmetric geometry, where the authors explicitly state pure 2D imaging is inadequate — an Intel RealSense D405 depth camera feeding SVM/Random Forest models reached a markedly lower R² = 0.786 for volume and R² = 0.849 for mass (abstract-level access only; full text paywalled) ([ScienceDirect S0925521425001383](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925521425001383)). Because waste streams are mixed and irregular, a team should expect volume-estimation accuracy closer to the garlic-clove regime (R² ≈ 0.8) than the orange regime (R² ≈ 0.97) unless objects are regularized — flattened cardboard, cylindrical cans — though this is this report's own inference from cross-domain comparison, not a claim any cited source makes directly.

In waste and recycling specifically, depth is used for a **different purpose** than volume/mass proxying: grasp-priority and occlusion reasoning. A KUKA LBR iiwa cobot system pairing a Vision-Language-Action model with RGB-D sensing uses the depth channel to identify the topmost object in clutter and establish grasp ordering, with the authors describing depth as "indispensable for waste sorting in three-dimensional settings" — but this finding comes from search-engine synthesis of a paper the publisher blocked from direct fetch, not primary-text confirmation ([DOI 10.3390/robotics15050100](https://doi.org/10.3390/robotics15050100)). RGB-D fusion for construction-and-demolition waste classification, by contrast, adds depth as a fourth channel and gains only a modest +1.9–2.3 percentage points over RGB alone, with the main claimed benefit being robustness to dust and lighting rather than accuracy (abstract-level access) ([ScienceDirect S0956053X21006668](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0956053X21006668)). **No source found in this research combines a depth foundation model with volume or mass estimation specifically for a recycling or waste-sorting system for the purpose of value proxying** — a team pursuing this would be combining two currently-separate literatures (produce-grading volumetrics and waste-sorting RGB-D grasping). Nor was mass-from-vision-alone, without a physical scale or density assumption, demonstrated with strong accuracy anywhere: even the orange paper's "mass" number is a load-cell reading, not a vision estimate. A density-lookup approach — estimated volume × an assumed density-by-material-class, without a physical scale — was not found evidenced in any sorting or recycling paper in this research; this is a specific, concrete, and apparently unaddressed technique the team could implement and report on as an original contribution.

## Recyclable prices swing violently, and value-weighted throughput is unclaimed territory

US curbside recyclable commodity prices are tracked regularly by trade press (*Resource Recycling*) and regional bodies (NERC), and both sources agree that 2025 was a rout across nearly every material grade. The December 2025 year-end NERC snapshot and the June 2025 Resource Recycling snapshot, read together, show just how fast these numbers move within a single year:

| Steel cans | $158.75/ton (–8.7% YoY) | $168/ton |
| Natural HDPE | 46.81 ¢/lb (down ~1/3 YoY) | 69.44 ¢/lb |

| Mixed glass | **–$25.31/ton (negative — a cost to process)** | not reported |

*([NERC, "2025 Recycling Markets Took a Beating in a Weak Retail Year"](https://www.nerc.org/2025-recycling-markets-took-a-beating-in-a-weak-retail-year); [Resource Recycling, Jun 2025](https://resource-recycling.com/recycling/2025/06/17/all-major-curbside-grades-drop-in-value/))*

PET fell from 14.59¢/lb in June 2025 to 5.38¢/lb by December — a further ~63% decline in just six months, on top of a ~65% year-over-year drop, making it the steepest-declining grade tracked. January 2025 had shown HDPE and PET *rising* while fiber grades held stable ([Resource Recycling, Jan 2025](https://resource-recycling.com/recycling/2025/01/14/january-brings-rising-hdpe-and-pet-prices-fiber-stability/)), so the direction reversed twice within a single year — this is not a market with a stable trend to hardcode. NERC's narrative attributes the 2025 collapse to a K-shaped economic recovery suppressing packaging demand and paper-mill closures, with only "modest stabilization" projected for 2026 as older processing capacity exits ([NERC](https://www.nerc.org/2025-recycling-markets-took-a-beating-in-a-weak-retail-year)). Internationally, recycled HDPE in Q1 2025 ranged from roughly $1,591/metric ton in the US to $1,280 in Germany and $915 in India, per a commercial market-research aggregator whose underlying data was not independently verified ([IMARC Group](https://www.imarcgroup.com/recycled-high-density-polyethylene-pricing-report)). The clearest design implication is that **mixed glass carries a negative value** — processors pay a tipping fee to accept it — so any economic value-weighted sorting policy must be able to represent negative-value items as a cost to avoid or deprioritize, not merely rank a list of positive-value materials; and because grade prices can move 30–65% within a single year, any value-weighted metric the team builds should treat price as an explicit, timestamped, configurable input rather than a constant baked into the code.

This leads directly to the most consequential open question this research examined: **does a throughput or reward metric that weights sorting decisions by economic material value, rather than pure accuracy or count, already exist in the literature?** This was searched deliberately and thoroughly, and not found at the level of an individual, real-time, vision-driven pick decision. The largest real-world precedent located — Google's fleet of 23 RL-enabled mobile manipulators, deployed over 24 months across three office buildings, accumulating 9,527 hours of real-world robotic experience and validated with 4,800 evaluation trials — assigns reward per-episode for correct bin placement, with no economic or material-value weighting in its formulation ([arXiv 2305.03270](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03270)). A delta-robot scrap-metal sorting paper using TD3/SAC/PPO reports 89% accuracy alongside a 51% throughput increase in simulation, but its exact reward formulation — whether it includes any value term — could not be extracted from the available text, so this remains a genuinely open item the team could resolve by reading the full PDF directly ([arXiv 2406.13453](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.13453)). Two circularity-focused RL frameworks, CIRO7.2 and CiRL, weight disassembly decisions by a material **criticality/scarcity coefficient** derived from thermodynamic modeling (e.g., 0.1 versus 0.95 for two materials in one test case) — a related but genuinely different axis from market price per kilogram ([arXiv 2506.11748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11748); [arXiv 2505.21536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21536)). The single closest-sounding title match, "The value of information in automated sorting... " (*Journal of Industrial and Production Engineering*, 2026), was checked directly and confirmed — at the abstract level, full text blocked by the publisher — to mean decision-theoretic value of *look-ahead information* about future conveyor states, not economic material value, explicitly ruling it out as prior art ([DOI 10.1080/21681015.2026.2638174](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/21681015.2026.2638174)).

Economic optimization using real material prices does exist in this industry, but one level removed from where the team is proposing to work: a materials-recovery-facility process-optimization patent feeds daily material prices, labor costs, and required volume into a program that computes optimal feed-rate and staffing to maximize net revenue over a shift ([USPTO 7994448](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7994448)) — genuine price-informed optimization, but at the level of *facility configuration over a shift*, not an *individual vision-based pick decision in real time*. Separately, classical queueing theory already models the conveyor-feeder problem the team will face: a 2D Poisson process for intermittent-motion feeding, or an M/G/1 queue with customer impatience for continuous-motion feeding, where an unpicked item passing the reach zone is analogous to a queue "reneging," with named failure modes of **starvation** (no items available) and **saturation** (too many items, causing missed picks) ([ScienceDirect S0736584599000411](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0736584599000411); [IEEE Xplore 727298](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/727298/)). Knapsack-style formulations are used for resource-constrained item selection in adjacent warehouse-robotics contexts, and grasp-confidence-weighted queue reordering — using probability of successful pick as a per-item priority weight — is already published in warehouse item-picking research ([ResearchGate 321821478](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321821478_Task_prioritization_for_automated_robotic_item_picking)). Combining that pattern — a per-item scalar weight reordering a throughput-constrained pick queue — with market price per kilogram instead of (or multiplied by) pick-success probability is a coherent, technically well-grounded construction, but this exact combination was not found published anywhere in robotics, reinforcement learning, circular-economy, or operations-research literature searched. Based on that deliberate, cross-literature search coming up empty — an absence-of-evidence finding, not proof of non-existence — **a market-price-weighted, throughput-constrained sorting metric applied to real-time, per-item pick decisions is a genuine and well-scoped opportunity for a novel contribution**, not a rediscovery of existing work.

## Synthetic training data helps only when anchored to real belt imagery

### Copy-paste and SAM-based compositing are proven pieces, never assembled for waste

Copy-Paste augmentation — randomly pasting object instances, with their masks, from one image onto another — was introduced by Ghiasi et al. (CVPR 2021) and shown, in a fully-verified primary source, to lift a Cascade R-CNN baseline from 54.8 to 57.3 box AP / 46.9 to 49.1 mask AP on COCO test-dev, with gains concentrated disproportionately on rare/long-tail categories in LVIS (+6.1 mask AP on rare classes versus +3.7 on common ones) ([arXiv 2012.07177](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07177), verified via ar5iv). An ablation in that same paper found that realistic scene context is not required — roughly 46.8% of generated images pasted objects into semantically mismatched scenes, yet performance held — which matters directly for a belt pipeline, since it suggests careful background matching may be less important than simply having enough diverse instances. X-Paste (ICML 2023) extends the same structural pattern — automatic instance-mask generation, followed by a filtering step to discard low-quality masks, before pasting — but sources its objects from CLIP-filtered web images and Stable Diffusion rather than real photos, evaluated on COCO/LVIS rather than waste data (abstract-level figures, not independently verified: +1.2 box/+1.2 mask AP over standard copy-paste, larger gains on LVIS rare classes) ([arXiv 2212.03863](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.03863)).

In construction waste specifically, Ji et al. (PLOS ONE, Jan 2024) built a directly-fetched, fully-quantified copy-paste pipeline: RGB-D cameras auto-extracted individual waste-object contours, a distribution-points algorithm computed paste positions, and a Mask R-CNN detector was trained on the result. Under complex, stacked conditions, the copy-paste-synthetic training set reached **97.74 F1**, beating the manually-labeled-only baseline's 85.97 F1 by nearly 12 points — the synthetic data's largest edge appeared precisely under the clutter conditions a conveyor belt presents ([PMC10790992](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10790992/)). This directly **contradicts** WasteGAN's characterization of copy-paste, in its own related-work section, as having "limited object variance" and being "ineffective for cluttered scenes" ([arXiv 2409.16999](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16999)) — a framing from a competing method's authors, not an independent benchmark. The most plausible reconciliation, drawn from comparing the two studies rather than stated by either, is that the discriminating factor is source-object diversity and compositing realism, not the copy-paste mechanism itself: Ji et al. sourced real RGB-D captures at reasonable scale (626 and 302 real images expanded to 2,386 and 1,130 synthetic images respectively) and pasted onto real backgrounds, while the negative characterizations trace to setups with far more limited object variety.

No published, waste-specific paper was found that does what the team is likely considering: using SAM or SAM2 to auto-segment objects out of single-object waste photos, in order to composite them onto cluttered or belt-style backgrounds at controlled density and occlusion, exporting both images and instance masks for training. Every building block exists separately — WasteSAM, a SAM variant fine-tuned for construction-waste segmentation on 15,000+ masks, reportedly outperforms vanilla SAM by 23.9% in Dice similarity (abstract-level figures, not independently verified) ([DOI 10.1177/0734242x241290743](https://doi.org/10.1177/0734242x241290743)); ParticleSAM adapts SAM's Automatic Mask Generator for segmenting small particles in shredded material streams ([arXiv 2508.03490](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03490)) — but both apply SAM to direct segmentation of real cluttered photos, not as a front-end for synthetic compositing. Generic, non-waste tooling confirms the technique is buildable: an open Ultralytics pull request implements "CopyPasteWithAutoSegmentation," using SAM to auto-generate masks so standard copy-paste can run without hand-drawn annotations ([Ultralytics PR #13755](https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/pull/13755)). The one waste paper that does cutout-compositing at all, Behbahani et al. (2026), used MediaPipe Hands — not SAM — to extract hand-and-forearm regions from roughly 100 base objects, and got negative results (below). A SAM/SAM2-based extraction-and-composite pipeline for waste is therefore **methodologically well-supported but empirically untested** — a concrete, well-scoped, and genuinely citable gap the team is positioned to fill.

### The domain gap is severe, and synthetic-only training does not close it

The gap between clean, single-object waste datasets and cluttered real-world scenes is large and documented at multiple levels. At the classification level, a model trained *only* on TrashNet's controlled white-posterboard backgrounds collapsed from 94.18% in-domain accuracy to **41.04%** zero-shot top-1 accuracy on RealWaste, a real landfill-imagery dataset — a drop of more than 53 percentage points, recovered to 79.67% only after fine-tuning ([PMC13366473](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13366473/)). At the detection level, the authors of the ZeroWaste dataset state plainly that models trained on "simplified datasets with solid background and little to no clutter" cannot be used for automated detection on real conveyor-belt imagery, and that popular detection and segmentation architectures "struggle to generalize" to their real, cluttered data even when evaluated in-domain — TridentNet, Mask R-CNN, and RetinaNet reach only 21–24 AP, and DeepLabV3+ only 52.13 mIoU, on real material even when trained on matched real data ([arXiv 2106.02740](https://arxiv.org/html/2106.02740)). **A dataset-size note the team should carry forward carefully**: the original ZeroWaste paper (Bashkirova et al., CVPR 2022) reports ZeroWaste-f as **3,002 training / 572 validation / 929 test images (4,661 total annotated frames), 4 material classes** (cardboard, soft plastic, rigid plastic, metal), plus a separate unlabeled ZeroWaste-s pool of 6,212 images for semi-supervised methods — this is the dataset's own authoritative sizing. The detector-comparison paper cited in the previous section (arXiv 2508.18799) reports a different count for what it also calls "ZeroWaste-f" — 4,503 labeled images and 27,000 annotated instances, with cardboard exceeding 66% of annotations and metal under 2%. This research could not reconcile the two counts; they are reported here attributed to their respective sources rather than merged into one number, and a team citing ZeroWaste-f's size should use the original dataset paper's 3,002/572/929 figures and cite the class-imbalance statistic separately to arXiv 2508.18799.

Clutter degrades performance continuously, not as a binary clean-versus-cluttered switch: SortWaste (arXiv 2601.02299) introduces "ClutterScore," a weighted combination of class entropy, object count, size entropy, and bounding-box overlap, and reports detection performance degrading steadily above a ClutterScore of 0.4 — though the specific Table 5 figures retrieved (high-clutter subset mAP@0.50 ≈ 0.60 versus full-dataset ≈ 0.59) do not actually show a drop and mildly contradict the paper's own narrative, an internal inconsistency this research could not resolve; the qualitative degradation trend, not the specific 0.60-versus-0.59 figures, is what should be relied on. A second, independently-collected in-the-wild litter dataset, TACO, reinforces the same low-absolute-mAP pattern on real cluttered imagery: a 2025 NAS-based detector study on a curated 5-class TACO subset reports state-of-the-art results still capping out at 15.9–19.5 mAP50 across TrashDet-l, AltiDet-m, Deformable DETR, YOLOv8m, SWDet-m, and YOLOv5m, attributed explicitly to realistic heterogeneous backgrounds and a mix of small, deformable objects ([arXiv 2512.20746](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.20746)) — an independent confirmation that ZeroWaste's low-20s in-domain AP is not a one-off but characteristic of real cluttered waste imagery generally, though a semi-supervised ensemble approach on ZeroWaste itself has since reached 51.6 mAP, suggesting meaningful in-domain progress since 2022 ([arXiv 2508.18799](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18799)).

The clearest test of whether synthetic compositing can substitute for real target-domain data is the **VisDA 2022 Challenge**, built exactly around this question: a procedurally-generated synthetic dataset — **SynthWaste, verified at 2,099 frames** (an earlier-circulating figure of 20,990 frames was checked against this primary source and found incorrect) — of recyclable objects randomly spawned onto a conveyor belt in Unity, benchmarked against the real ZeroWaste conveyor-belt dataset ([arXiv 2303.14828](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.14828)). Training on SynthWaste alone reached only 20.4% mIoU on the real target set; adding real ZeroWaste-v1 data alongside it raised that to 45.5% — still short of the 52.26% mIoU a real-only baseline achieved with no synthetic data at all. The paper's headline, directly-quoted finding is blunt: **"a small real dataset is better than a large synthetic one."** Even real-to-real transfer proved harder than expected — a model trained on ZeroWaste-v1 and tested on ZeroWaste-v2 (different facility, different season, both real) dropped 17.29 mIoU points for DeepLabv2 and 10.41 for SegFormer, showing that domain shift is severe even without a synthetic component. A second, independent test reinforces the same caution: Behbahani et al. (2026), building a real 148-photo campus-waste dataset alongside a 695-image synthetic/derived pool generated from only about 100 base objects, found that **every synthetic or derived training configuration underperformed real-data-only training** on YOLOv8n — real-only reached mAP@0.5 = 0.691, while the full combined augmentation pipeline dropped to 0.487 — with the authors themselves attributing the failure to their small object pool overstating true diversity ([arXiv 2607.19535](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19535)). Read together with Ji et al.'s positive copy-paste result above, the pattern across all four studies is consistent: synthetic data helped when it was anchored to real target-domain images at reasonable object-source diversity (Ji et al.; SynthWaste-plus-real), and failed when used alone or built from too narrow an object pool (SynthWaste-alone; Behbahani et al.) — a cross-study inference, not a claim stated by any single source, but one supported by all four data points pointing the same direction. The actionable conclusion for the team: plan on synthetic compositing, including any SAM-based pipeline they build, as a **supplement to a meaningful real, belt-collected image set**, not a replacement for one, and prioritize the diversity of source objects over the sophistication of the compositing pipeline.

Beyond copy-paste, the waste literature's other class-imbalance and occlusion techniques are thinner. GAN-based rebalancing (WasteGAN) reports a +5.8% mIoU gain over a 100-real-sample baseline and specifically improves label-distribution preservation "for underrepresented classes like metal and rigid plastic" ([arXiv 2409.16999](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16999)); Mixup and CutMix are explicitly flagged by one paper as recommended future work rather than already-validated techniques in production waste settings ([PMC13366473](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13366473/)); and location-aware occlusion-specific copy-paste, which places pasted objects to create realistic occlusion patterns, is imported from the general computer-vision literature and has not yet been benchmarked on a waste-specific dataset ([arXiv 2310.17949](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.17949)). No comprehensive survey of data augmentation for waste detection specifically was located in this research.

## Conclusion

No single model closes this problem, and the evidence base itself has a shape worth naming: almost every hard number attached to a *deployed* system in this research is vendor-published or vendor-authored, while almost every number that survives independent, peer-reviewed scrutiny comes from a benchmark dataset (ZeroWaste-f, TACO, SortWaste) that is deliberately harder and lower-scoring than any commercial pitch would suggest. A team that measures its own pipeline honestly — reporting mAP on its own held-out belt footage with the same rigor this research demanded of AMP's and Recycleye's marketing claims — will likely produce numbers less flattering than the industry's, but more defensible, and that gap between vendor claims and independently-measured reality is itself worth stating explicitly in the paper's related-work section rather than smoothed over.

Two threads reward one more targeted check before the team commits to their strongest claims. First, Koskinopoulou et al.'s full text remains the single highest-value item to retrieve — it is the paper's most citable peer-reviewed anchor, but currently a blank check, good only for "deployed and assessed at a real plant" until someone with institutional access reads past the abstract. Second, before finalizing any claim of novelty for a value-weighted sorting metric, the team should read the full text of the delta-robot scrap-metal RL paper (arXiv 2406.13453) directly — its reward formulation could not be extracted in this research due to a fetch-size limit, not because it was checked and ruled out, which is a meaningfully weaker basis for exclusion than the "value of information" paper, which was read and definitively shown to answer a different question. With those two checks done, the architecture this research supports — nano-YOLO or RT-DETR detection, ByteTrack tracking, SAM2 reserved for actuator-precision mask refinement, open-vocabulary models kept offline as a labeling tool, metric depth for volume (not yet mass) proxying, and a market-price-weighted throughput objective the literature search did not find anyone else building — gives a four-person team a full semester's worth of well-scoped, individually tractable, and honestly novel engineering work.
