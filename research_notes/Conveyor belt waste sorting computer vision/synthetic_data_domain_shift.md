# Copy-Paste Augmentation, Synthetic Scene Composition, and Domain Shift for Waste/Recyclables Detection

## What is "Copy-Paste augmentation" and how has it been applied to waste/trash/recyclables detection specifically?

### Takeaway
Copy-Paste augmentation (Ghiasi et al., CVPR 2021) is a simple instance-level augmentation — randomly pasting object instances (with their masks) from one image onto another — that gave solid, generalizable gains for instance segmentation/detection without extra training or inference cost. It has since been adapted for waste-adjacent domains (construction/solid-waste detection, remote-sensing solid waste), where copy-paste-generated synthetic images have **matched or exceeded manually-labeled training data**, especially under cluttered/stacked conditions — but very little of this work targets small consumer recyclables on a conveyor belt specifically; most waste-domain copy-paste work is in construction/demolition waste or aerial/remote-sensing solid-waste detection.

### Cited Findings
- **Original method**: Ghiasi et al., "Simple Copy-Paste is a Strong Data Augmentation Method for Instance Segmentation," CVPR 2021. Randomly select two images, apply random scale jittering (0.1–2.0×, "large-scale jittering") and horizontal flip to each, then paste a random subset of object instances from one onto the other; composite via alpha-blending with Gaussian filtering (but ablations show blending vs. no blending gives identical 50.0 box AP / 43.7 mask AP — blending is not necessary). On COCO test-dev this produced 57.3 box AP / 49.1 mask AP, a +1.5 box AP / +0.6 mask AP improvement over the prior state of the art, using a Cascade R-CNN baseline (54.8 box AP / 46.9 mask AP) where Copy-Paste alone added +1.2 box AP / +0.5 mask AP, and combined with self-training added +2.5 box AP / +2.2 mask AP. On LVIS v1.0 (long-tailed/rare categories), an EfficientNet-B7 NAS-FPN model reached 38.1 mask AP (+3.4), with rare-category AP (APr) of 32.1 — +3.6 mask APr over the 2020 LVIS Challenge winner — and gains of +6.1 mask AP on rare vs. +3.7 mask AP on common categories, i.e., copy-paste disproportionately helps rare/long-tail classes. An ablation also found ~46.8% of generated images paste objects into semantically mismatched scenes (e.g., indoor objects onto outdoor backgrounds) yet performance remained strong, indicating realistic surrounding context is not required. — [arXiv:2012.07177](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07177) (full text verified via [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2012.07177))
- **Follow-up — X-Paste** (Zhao et al., ICML 2023) scales Copy-Paste to open/large vocabularies by sourcing instances from CLIP-filtered web images and Stable-Diffusion-generated images, then using automatic instance-mask generation + an instance-filtering module to remove low-quality masks (i.e., replacing manual mask annotation with an automated mask-generation + filtering pipeline — structurally the same role SAM could play). Using CenterNet2/ResNet50, reported as achieving +1.2 box AP / +1.2 mask AP over standard Copy-Paste on COCO (57.0 box mAP / 48.6 mask mAP), with larger gains on LVIS rare categories: +3.2 box AP / +4.4 mask AP, reaching 44.4 mask mAP (all) / 43.3 mask mAP (rare) (figures from abstract/index summary; full text not fetched in this session, so treat as directionally reliable but unverified against primary text). — [arXiv:2212.03863](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.03863), [ICML PMLR version](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/zhao23f/zhao23f.pdf)
- **Follow-up — occlusion-focused copy-paste**: "Instance Segmentation under Occlusions via Location-aware Copy-Paste Data Augmentation" explicitly designs paste locations to create realistic occlusion patterns rather than fully random placement. — [arXiv:2310.17949](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.17949). Similarly, "Occlusion Copy & Paste" targets occluded human instance segmentation by synthesizing occluders without requiring new human labels. — [arXiv:2210.03686](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.03686)
- **Waste/construction-domain application #1 — copy-paste + GAN hybrid for remote-sensing solid waste**: A 2022 IEEE paper combines a modified pix2pix model (with a local-global discriminator to fix the blurriness of standard pix2pix outputs) with a copy-paste operator that pastes generated waste-object bounding boxes into different background images, to expand training data for solid-waste detection in remote sensing imagery, validated on FPN and YOLOv4. — [IEEE Xplore 9904838](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9904838/) (details from abstract/search index; full text not retrieved — paywalled)
- **Waste/construction-domain application #2 — copy-paste for stacked construction waste (quantitative before/after)**: Ji et al. (PLOS ONE, Jan 2024) built a copy-paste pipeline for stacked construction solid waste: RGB-D cameras auto-extract individual waste-object contours (depth thresholding + topological analysis), then a distribution-points algorithm computes paste positions on background images, with added rotation/flip/lighting augmentation. Detector: Mask R-CNN (ResNet101 + FPN, COCO-pretrained, TensorFlow 1.15). Dataset sizes: 626 sparse single-category real images (Aauto) → expanded to 2,386 synthetic copy-paste images (ACP); 302 interference-condition real images (Bauto) → expanded to 1,130 synthetic images (BCP); a manually-labeled baseline of 1,884 real images (Amanual); test sets of 777 "simple condition" and 336 "complex condition" images. **Results (F1-score)**: simple conditions — manual-only 94.81 vs. copy-paste-synthetic 95.98; complex/stacked conditions — manual-only 85.97 vs. copy-paste-synthetic **97.74**, i.e., synthetic copy-paste data exceeded manually-labeled performance, with the largest gain precisely under cluttered/stacked/occluded conditions. — [PLOS ONE / PMC10790992](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10790992/)
- **Contrasting result**: WasteGAN (see Key Question 4) characterizes copy-paste, as used in prior waste-sorting work, as having "limited object variance" and being "ineffective for cluttered scenes" — but this is the WasteGAN authors' framing of competing work in their related-work section, not an independent benchmark; it directly conflicts with the Ji et al. finding above (see Key Question 4 for full discussion of this contradiction and the likely explanation — object/source diversity, not the copy-paste mechanism itself). — [arXiv:2409.16999](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16999)

### Inferences
- The core Copy-Paste mechanism itself is domain-agnostic and simple to implement; its published waste-domain applications lean toward construction/demolition/remote-sensing solid waste (piles, aerial imagery) rather than small consumer recyclables (bottles, cans, wrappers) on a conveyor belt, which is a gap directly relevant to the user's TrashNet/Roboflow-style use case.
- Because Copy-Paste's biggest documented wins (in the general CV literature and in Ji et al.) are for rare/long-tail classes and cluttered/stacked scenes, it is plausible it would help class-imbalanced, occlusion-heavy waste-sorting datasets — but this needs domain-specific validation, since the WasteGAN vs. Ji et al. contradiction shows results are not guaranteed to transfer.

### Gaps
- No full-text access to the IEEE remote-sensing pix2pix+copy-paste paper (9904838) — quantitative before/after mAP numbers for that specific study were not retrievable (paywalled).
- No paper was found applying vanilla Simple Copy-Paste (Ghiasi et al.) directly and specifically to small recyclables/conveyor-belt detection with reported mAP deltas — the closest analogues are construction-waste (Ji et al.) and general instance-segmentation literature.

## Has anyone used SAM or SAM2 to auto-segment objects from single-object waste images in order to synthetically composite them onto cluttered/belt backgrounds at varying density and occlusion, generating both images and instance masks for training?

### Takeaway
**No published, waste-specific paper was found that does exactly this** (SAM/SAM2 auto-segmenting single-object waste photos → compositing onto cluttered/conveyor-belt backgrounds at controlled density/occlusion → exporting images + instance masks for detector training). The building blocks all exist separately and independently — SAM/SAM2 is used in the waste-recycling literature for segmentation and mask-refinement tasks (including a fine-tuned "WasteSAM" and "ParticleSAM"), and general-purpose (non-waste) open-source tooling already implements "SAM-powered copy-paste" — but no paper in the searched waste-detection literature reports combining them into a compositing pipeline of the kind the user describes. This is a genuine, citable gap in the literature, not just an absence of search results.

### Cited Findings
- **WasteSAM** (Heo & Na, *Waste Management & Research*, 2025): a SAM variant fine-tuned specifically for construction-waste image segmentation, trained on a dataset reported as 15,000+ masks across five construction-material categories, said to outperform vanilla SAM by an average of 23.9% in Dice similarity coefficient and 30.0% in normalized surface distance (figures from abstract/index summary; full text not fetched in this session, so treat as directionally reliable but unverified against primary text). This is used for **segmentation accuracy on real construction-waste photos**, not for extracting isolated single objects to re-composite into synthetic cluttered scenes. — [DOI: 10.1177/0734242x241290743](https://doi.org/10.1177/0734242x241290743)
- **ParticleSAM**: adapts SAM's Automatic Mask Generator (ViT-B backbone) as a baseline for segmenting small particles in crushed/shredded recycling-process material streams, for material-quality monitoring — again a direct segmentation application on real cluttered material, not a synthetic-compositing pipeline. — [arXiv:2508.03490](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.03490)
- **SAM vs. SAM2 comparison for e-waste**: a study evaluates both SAM and SAM2 for segmenting irregular components in e-waste disassembly imagery, assessing whether SAM2's architectural updates (streaming/memory-attention for video) improve segmentation accuracy for these objects — again a direct-segmentation-quality study, not a synthetic-compositing study. — [arXiv:2603.27441](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.27441)
- **General-purpose (non-waste) tooling that matches the user's described technique**: an open pull request to the Ultralytics YOLO codebase implements "CopyPasteWithAutoSegmentation" — using SAM to auto-generate instance masks for otherwise-unlabeled/unmasked images so that standard Copy-Paste augmentation can be applied without hand-drawn segmentation masks (grid-point or class-agnostic-detection prompting, then SAM segments each detected object). This is a generic, non-waste-specific implementation, confirming the technique is feasible and has been built as tooling, but is not a published research study with waste-specific benchmark results. — [Ultralytics PR #13755](https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics/pull/13755)
- **Closest published methodological analogue (non-SAM)**: X-Paste (see Key Question 1) already performs the same *structural* role — automatic instance-mask generation from filtered images, followed by an instance-filtering step to discard bad masks, before pasting — but sources its images from CLIP+Stable Diffusion rather than from single-object waste photos, and is evaluated on COCO/LVIS, not a waste dataset. — [arXiv:2212.03863](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.03863)
- **A paper that composites cutouts onto backgrounds for waste detection but explicitly does NOT use SAM**: "Synthetic and Derived Training Images for Campus Waste Detection: A Multi-Seed Evaluation with YOLOv8n" (Behbahani et al., 2026) generates synthetic training images by pasting object cutouts onto new backgrounds, but used **MediaPipe Hands** (not SAM/SAM2) to extract hand-and-forearm regions for compositing, starting from ~100 base waste objects on plain backgrounds. See Key Question 4 for their (negative) detection results. — [arXiv:2607.19535](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19535)

### Inferences
- The absence of a dedicated SAM/SAM2-based waste-compositing paper is likely a timing/niche gap rather than a technical barrier: SAM (2023) and SAM2 (2024) are recent, waste-detection research groups have so far applied them to direct segmentation of real cluttered scenes (WasteSAM, ParticleSAM) rather than as a data-generation front-end, and the one waste paper that does cutout-compositing (Behbahani et al.) used a simpler hand/object-specific tool (MediaPipe) instead, likely because their source objects were hand-held items rather than isolated single-object catalog-style photos.
- Given X-Paste's demonstrated pattern (auto-mask-generation → filtering → paste, with disproportionate gains on rare/long-tail categories) and TrashNet/Roboflow-style datasets' single-object, high-quality-mask-friendly nature, a SAM/SAM2-based extraction step feeding a Copy-Paste-style compositor is a methodologically well-supported but empirically untested idea for waste detection specifically.

### Gaps
- No waste-specific paper combining SAM/SAM2 mask extraction from single-object images with density/occlusion-controlled compositing onto belt/cluttered backgrounds, and no instance-mask-inclusive synthetic dataset of this kind was found in the literature searched. This should be flagged to the report as a specific, actionable research gap rather than an established technique.
- Could not determine whether any unpublished/industry pipelines (e.g., commercial recycling-AI vendors) do this, since such work would likely not appear in academic search results.

## What published evidence exists on the domain gap between clean/single-object waste datasets and cluttered real-world detection performance — i.e., how much does mAP drop when a model trained on clean data is tested on cluttered/real scenes?

### Takeaway
The domain gap is large and is explicitly documented at both the **classification** level (a model trained only on TrashNet — controlled white-posterboard background — collapsed from 94.18% in-domain accuracy to 41.04% zero-shot accuracy on RealWaste, a real landfill-imagery dataset) and the **detection/segmentation** level (the ZeroWaste dataset authors state plainly that models trained on "simplified datasets with solid background and little to no clutter" cannot be used for automated detection on real waste-processing-plant conveyor belts, and report that popular detection/segmentation architectures reach only 21–24 AP / ~44–52 mIoU even when trained and tested in-domain on real cluttered data). Within-dataset clutter also degrades performance continuously, not just as a binary clean-vs-cluttered gap.

### Cited Findings
- **Quantified classification domain gap (TrashNet → RealWaste)**: "The LCNet-0.5 student (trained only on TrashNet) reaches 41.04% top-1 accuracy and macro-F1 0.3648 on RealWaste, despite reaching 94.18% on the TrashNet test set" — a >53 percentage-point drop. The authors attribute this to TrashNet's controlled white-posterboard background vs. RealWaste's realistic landfill imagery with cluttered backgrounds and partial occlusions (RealWaste: 4,752 images across 9 material types collected at an authentic landfill). Short fine-tuning on a two-stage schedule recovered performance to 79.67% accuracy / macro-F1 0.7929 on RealWaste. — Echchidmi & Bouayad, *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence*, June 2026, [PMC13366473](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13366473/)
- **ZeroWaste authors' explicit domain-gap statement**: "The domain shift between the simplified datasets with solid background and little to no clutter and the real images of the conveyor belt from the MRF, as well as the object-centric nature of the existing datasets, makes it impossible to use models trained on these datasets for automated detection on real waste processing plants," and that "popular detection and segmentation methods ... struggle to generalize to our data, which indicates [the] challenging nature of our in-the-wild dataset." — Bashkirova et al., CVPR 2022, [arXiv:2106.02740](https://arxiv.org/html/2106.02740)
- **ZeroWaste in-domain baseline detection/segmentation results** (i.e., the ceiling achievable even when training and testing on the same real cluttered distribution — illustrating how hard real cluttered waste detection is in absolute terms): object detection AP — TridentNet 24.2, Mask R-CNN 22.8, RetinaNet 21.0; semantic segmentation mIoU — DeepLabV3+ (fully supervised) 52.13, ReCo (semi-supervised) 44.12. Dataset: ZeroWaste-f, 3,002 train / 572 val / 929 test images (4,661 total annotated frames), 4 material classes (cardboard, soft plastic, rigid plastic, metal); plus ZeroWaste-s, 6,212 unlabeled images for semi-supervised methods. — [arXiv:2106.02740](https://arxiv.org/html/2106.02740)
- **Clutter as a continuous (not just binary) degradation factor**: SortWaste (Inacio et al., WACV 2026 Workshop) introduces "ClutterScore" (a weighted combination of class entropy, normalized object count, size entropy, and bounding-box overlap, weights fit via Pearson correlation with mAP@50) and states in its narrative that "as ClutterScore rises above 0.4, detection performance degrades steadily," with a described decrease in precision and overall mAP@0.50 moving from the full test set to a high-clutter (ClutterScore 0.6–0.8) subset. **Note on an internal inconsistency**: the specific Table 5 figures retrieved for this note give high-clutter-subset mAP@0.50 ≈ 0.60 versus full-dataset mAP@0.50 ≈ 0.59 — i.e., the numbers as extracted do not show a drop and mildly contradict the paper's own degradation narrative. This discrepancy is unresolved (it may be an extraction error, a different baseline/model in Table 5 than assumed, or a subtlety in how the "full dataset" comparator is computed); the qualitative degradation trend is what should be relied on here, not the specific 0.60-vs-0.59 figures. Dataset: 5,261 images (3,705 train / 780 val / 776 test), 87,252 bounding boxes across 8 classes. Baseline full-class AP@50: YOLOv11 0.567, RetinaNet 0.594, Faster R-CNN 0.573, TridentNet 0.584; plastic-only (4-class) subset: YOLOv11 AP@50 0.753 (best). — [arXiv:2601.02299](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.02299)
- **Roboflow garbage-classification domain gap (qualitative)**: a study using a Roboflow garbage-classification dataset (6 classes: plastic, paper, cardboard, metal, glass, biodegradables) found the model frequently confused waste — particularly paper and biodegradables — with the background, because lab settings provide neutral backgrounds while real-world backgrounds (grass, dirt, pavement) visually mimic certain litter textures; the authors describe a "domain gap where model confidence drops when transitioning from curated datasets to messy, real-world environments." — [arXiv:2410.09975](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.09975) (numeric mAP values could not be extracted — full-text fetch failed twice; this finding is from search-indexed abstract/summary text only and should be treated as directionally reliable but not precisely quantified)
- **TACO — low absolute mAP on real in-the-wild litter, independent confirmation of the ZeroWaste pattern**: TACO (Proença & Simões, 2020) is an in-the-wild litter dataset (~1,500 images, 4,784 annotations, 60 categories, an unofficial expanded version has 3,736 images/8,419 annotations — these size figures are index/abstract-derived, the TACO paper itself was not fetched in full) collected across woods, roads, and beaches. — [arXiv:2003.06975](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.06975). A recent NAS-based detector study benchmarking a curated 5-class TACO subset (paper, plastic, bottle, can, cigarette) reports state-of-the-art mAP50 values that remain low in absolute terms — TrashDet-l 19.5, AltiDet-m 18.4, Deformable DETR 16.8, YOLOv8m 16.6, SWDet-m 16.4, YOLOv5m 15.9 — and explicitly attributes this ceiling to TACO's "realistic conditions, heterogeneous backgrounds, and mixture of small and deformable objects" plus class imbalance. This mirrors the ZeroWaste pattern (low-20s AP even in-domain) on a second, independent real-world cluttered dataset. — Tran & Hu, arXiv:2512.20746 (Dec 2025), full text fetched via [arXiv:2512.20746](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.20746)
- **Additional in-domain ZeroWaste detection result**: Abid, Muhammad & Khan (BMVC 2025) report 51.6 mAP on fine-tuned transformer-based detectors on ZeroWaste after their semi-supervised ensemble pseudo-labeling pipeline — notably higher than the original ZeroWaste-paper baselines (21–24 AP) above, suggesting substantial progress since 2022 on the in-domain (not cross-domain) cluttered-detection problem specifically. This figure is abstract-sourced (full text was not retrievable due to page-size limits), so the exact metric definition (AP50 vs. AP, which classes) is unconfirmed. — [arXiv:2508.18799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18799)

### Inferences
- The magnitude of domain-gap collapse (>50 points in classification accuracy) suggests that for detection tasks — which are strictly harder than classification because they must also localize under clutter/occlusion — the mAP drop from a TrashNet/Roboflow-style clean single-object training set to a real conveyor-belt test set is plausibly at least as severe, though no study directly measures "trained on isolated-object detection data, tested on cluttered belt detection data" with a reported mAP delta (see Gaps).
- The ZeroWaste in-domain AP ceiling (21–24 AP) being so low even without a cross-dataset domain-gap component implies that clutter/occlusion/deformability are themselves extremely hard, independent of the clean-to-cluttered transfer problem — i.e., even a model trained on realistic cluttered data struggles, so a model trained only on clean data would be expected to perform substantially worse still.

### Gaps
- No study was found that reports a direct **detection mAP** (not classification accuracy, not in-domain mIoU) comparison of "trained on a clean/single-object waste dataset (e.g., TrashNet-, TACO-, or Roboflow-style) → tested on a cluttered/conveyor-belt detection benchmark (e.g., ZeroWaste, SortWaste)." The 94.18%→41.04% figure is a strong, precisely-sourced proxy but is a classification (not detection) result on RealWaste (landfill imagery), not conveyor-belt imagery.
- Precise quantitative numbers from the Roboflow-garbage-classification domain-gap paper (arXiv:2410.09975) could not be retrieved due to repeated fetch failures; only qualitative/directional claims are available.
- TACO's own class-imbalance/long-tail characteristics are well-documented qualitatively (see Key Question 5), but a clean-vs-cluttered mAP delta specific to TACO was not found.

## Are there existing works that specifically benchmark models trained on synthetic/composited cluttered scenes versus real cluttered scenes for waste detection (a sim-to-real or domain-shift study)?

### Takeaway
Yes — the clearest, most directly relevant example is the **VisDA 2022 Challenge**, which is built exactly around this scenario: a large procedurally-generated synthetic dataset of recyclable objects spawned onto a conveyor belt (SynthWaste) versus the real ZeroWaste conveyor-belt dataset, with a public leaderboard of domain-adaptation results. Its headline, direct-quote finding — **"a small real dataset is better than a large synthetic one"** — is a load-bearing result for anyone considering a synthetic-compositing strategy. A second, independent study (Behbahani et al. 2026, campus waste detection) reinforces this caution: in their setup, **every synthetic/derived-data configuration underperformed real-data-only training**. A third study (Ji et al., construction waste; see Key Question 1) found the opposite — synthetic copy-paste data outperformed manual labeling. The discriminating factor across these conflicting results appears to be the diversity/fidelity of the underlying object sources and the realism of the compositing, not a general verdict on synthetic data.

### Cited Findings
- **VisDA 2022 Challenge (SynthWaste vs. ZeroWaste)**: organized by Bashkirova, Mishra, Lteif and 16 others (Boston University, Tel Aviv University). SynthWaste is a **procedurally generated synthetic dataset of 2,099 frames** (note: an earlier search-engine summary reported "20,990 frames" — this was checked against the primary source and is incorrect; the verified figure from the paper's full text is 2,099) of recyclable objects **"randomly spawned onto a conveyor belt using Unity,"** with randomized lighting (type/intensity/direction/color), camera angle/position, level of clutter, and object-class distribution; a texture-randomized variant (SynthWaste-aug) applies Domain-Aware Universal Style Transfer using the Flickr Material Database. — [arXiv:2303.14828](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.14828)
  - **Quantitative domain-adaptation results (semantic segmentation mIoU on real ZeroWaste-v2 target)**: training on SynthWaste alone → 20.4% mIoU; training on SynthWaste + real ZeroWaste-v1 → 45.5% mIoU; training on real ZeroWaste-v1 only (DAFormer baseline, source-only) → 52.26% mIoU; winning domain-adaptation submissions reached 59.66% (1st, "SIA_Adapt": DAFormer + ConvNeXt-L + pseudo-labeling + self-training + model-soup ensembling), 55.46% (2nd, "HRDA": multi-resolution training + scale-attention fusion), and 54.38% (3rd, "PICO++": student-teacher + EMA + semi-supervised contrastive loss). — [arXiv:2303.14828](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.14828)
  - **Explicit real-to-real domain-gap numbers** (different MRF facility/season, both real): DeepLabv2 dropped from 47.83% (ZeroWaste-v1) to 30.54% (ZeroWaste-v2), a 17.29-point mIoU gap; SegFormer dropped from 56.00% to 45.49%, a 10.41-point gap — showing that even real-to-real domain shift (different plant/season) is severe, and sim-to-real is harder still.
  - **Key direct-quote finding**: *"A small real dataset is better than a large synthetic one"* — omitting the sim-to-real adaptation step entirely and instead doing real-to-real domain adaptation (ZeroWaste-v1 → ZeroWaste-v2) outperformed the synthetic-data route. — [arXiv:2303.14828](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.14828)
- **Campus Waste Detection multi-seed study** (Behbahani, Javanmardi, Ahmed, Vathana, 2026): built a real dataset of 148 photos (86 train / 31 val / 31 test; 4 classes — glass 8, metal 28, paper 34, plastic 78 instances) and a synthetic/derived pool of 695 images generated from only ~100 base objects via background replacement, lighting changes, sensor noise, rotation/warping (cutouts extracted with MediaPipe Hands, not SAM). Using YOLOv8n: **real-only training reached mAP@0.5 = 0.691**; background-replacement synthetic data alone = 0.560; isolated-object synthetic images = 0.680 (roughly comparable to real); the full combined augmentation pipeline = **0.487** (a substantial decline). Explicit conclusion: **"No evaluated synthetic/derived configuration surpassed real-only training on the fixed 31-photo test set."** The authors themselves note that their image count "overstates object diversity" since many derived images reused the same ~100 source objects — i.e., this negative result is plausibly a source-diversity/scale failure specific to their small setup, not evidence that synthetic compositing cannot work for waste detection in general. — [arXiv:2607.19535](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19535)
- **WasteGAN** (Bacchin, Barcellona, Terreran, Ghidoni, Menegatti — U. Padova; Kiyokawa — Osaka U.; 2024): a different (GAN-based, not copy-paste) route to synthetic cluttered-scene generation for waste sorting, benchmarked on ZeroWaste (5 classes: paper, rigid plastic, cardboard, metal, soft plastic), using only 100 labeled real examples with synthetic-to-real augmentation ratios up to 25:1. Reported gains: +5.8% mIoU vs. 100-real-samples-only baseline; +2.2% mIoU on average vs. the prior SemanticGAN method across architectures; real-world robotic grasp success rate 0.35 (WasteGAN) vs. 0.29 (no augmentation); categorization accuracy 0.45 vs. 0.44 baseline; false-positive rate reduced from 0.3 to 0.2; and 20× faster generation than SemanticGAN. The authors characterize prior copy-paste approaches as having "limited object variance" and being "ineffective for cluttered scenes," and procedural generation as "proved ineffective" for this domain — though this characterization comes from WasteGAN's own related-work framing (a motivated comparison favoring their method) and conflicts with Ji et al.'s positive copy-paste result (Key Question 1), so should be read as one data point, not a consensus verdict. — [arXiv:2409.16999](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16999)
- **Reconciling the conflicting synthetic-data verdicts**: the two studies with the most favorable synthetic-data results (Ji et al.'s construction-waste copy-paste, and the VisDA SynthWaste+real combination reaching 45.5% mIoU) both either (a) sourced object cutouts from real RGB-D captures at reasonable scale (2,386/1,130 synthetic images derived from 626/302 real images) and pasted onto real backgrounds, or (b) combined synthetic pretraining with a meaningful amount of real target-domain data rather than using synthetic data alone. The negative results (Behbahani et al.; SynthWaste-alone at 20.4% mIoU) both involved either very limited source-object diversity (~100 objects) or a pure sim-only training regime with no real target-domain data. This is an inference from cross-study comparison, not a claim made explicitly by any single source.

### Inferences
- For a practitioner following the user's proposed pipeline (SAM-extracted objects from single-object waste photos composited onto belt backgrounds), the VisDA/SynthWaste and Behbahani results together suggest that **synthetic-only training is unlikely to close the domain gap on its own**; the literature consistently favors synthetic data as a *supplement* to at least some real target-domain data (VisDA's SynthWaste+real-v1 nearly matched real-v1-only performance; WasteGAN needed only 100 real images to anchor a much larger synthetic augmentation) rather than a full replacement.
- Source-object diversity (number of distinct underlying instances, not number of generated images) appears to be a stronger predictor of synthetic-data success than pipeline sophistication — both the weakest results (Behbahani) and one of the "ineffective" characterizations (WasteGAN's critique of copy-paste) trace back to limited object variety.

### Gaps
- No study was found benchmarking a SAM/SAM2-based compositing pipeline specifically (see Key Question 2) against real cluttered scenes — the sim-to-real studies that do exist (VisDA/SynthWaste, WasteGAN, Behbahani et al.) use Unity procedural generation, GANs, or MediaPipe-based cutouts respectively, not SAM/SAM2.
- The VisDA challenge's full technical reports for each competing team (beyond the top-3 summarized here) were not individually retrieved; there may be additional method-specific ablations (e.g., effect of varying synthetic clutter density specifically) in team-specific papers not covered by this search.

## What data augmentation strategies (beyond copy-paste) are recommended in the waste-detection literature for class imbalance and occlusion robustness?

### Takeaway
Beyond copy-paste, the waste-detection literature's best-sourced recommendations are: GAN-based synthetic generation (WasteGAN and related GAN-augmented classification work) specifically to rebalance underrepresented classes; "LMix," a domain-specific augmentation method targeting limited samples and class imbalance together; sample-mixing augmentations (Mixup, CutMix), explicitly flagged by one paper as promising future work rather than already-validated in this domain; and occlusion-specific copy-paste variants imported from the general CV literature (not yet waste-specific). Loss-function-level fixes (Focal Loss) and standard geometric/photometric augmentation are very likely used in practice but could not be traced to a specific citable waste-domain source in this research pass — see Gaps. No single comprehensive survey paper devoted to this exact question was found.

### Cited Findings
- **LMix**: an improved data-augmentation method proposed for household waste classification specifically to tackle limited training samples and class imbalance simultaneously, combined with attention mechanisms and edge-feature enhancement in the same pipeline. — referenced in search results on "Research on Waste Image Classification Algorithm Based on Improved YOLOv8," [MDPI 2227-7390/14/14/2511](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/14/14/2511)
- **Mixup/CutMix flagged as future work, not yet validated**: Echchidmi & Bouayad (2026) explicitly note that "sample-mixing methods like Mixup and CutMix should be evaluated as controlled ablations, particularly for handling minority classes in production scenarios" — i.e., these are recommended directions rather than techniques with already-published waste-specific results in this paper. — [PMC13366473](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13366473/)
- **GAN-based augmentation for class rebalancing**: WasteGAN (see Key Question 4) specifically highlights improved label-distribution preservation "particularly for underrepresented classes like metal and rigid plastic" as a core advantage over both copy-paste and SemanticGAN. A separate "comprehensive approach for waste management with GAN-augmented classification" paper also reports on GAN-based augmentation for waste classification. — [arXiv:2409.16999](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.16999); [PMC12453751](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12453751/)
- **Reinforcement-learning-boosted augmentation**: a solid-waste classification approach combining reinforcement learning with data augmentation and hyperparameter optimization has been published (Scientific Reports), though the specific augmentation techniques used were not independently verified in this research pass. — [PMC13254286](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13254286/), [Nature/Sci Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-46347-7)
- **Location-aware occlusion-specific copy-paste** (already covered under Key Question 1) is the most directly occlusion-targeted augmentation technique found, even though it is not waste-specific: it places pasted objects to deliberately create realistic occlusion patterns rather than pasting at fully random locations. — [arXiv:2310.17949](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.17949)

### Inferences
- The recommendations split into two distinct levels: **loss-function-level** fixes for class imbalance (Focal Loss, Equalization-style losses — well established in the general long-tail detection literature per the LVIS-related papers surfaced during this search, e.g. [arXiv:2006.10408](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.10408), [arXiv:2012.08548](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.08548), [arXiv:2104.00885](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.00885), though not confirmed as applied specifically to a waste dataset) versus **data-level** fixes (copy-paste, GANs, LMix, Mixup/CutMix). A robust pipeline likely needs both, since data augmentation alone does not change the loss landscape's sensitivity to rare classes.
- Occlusion-specific augmentation techniques in this space (location-aware copy-paste, image infill) are largely imported from the general computer-vision literature rather than developed natively for waste/recyclables; no waste-specific paper was found that proposes or benchmarks a novel occlusion-augmentation technique on a waste dataset with quantitative deltas.

### Gaps
- No dedicated, comprehensive survey paper on "data augmentation for waste/recyclables detection" (covering class imbalance and occlusion together) was located; the findings above are assembled from scattered mentions across multiple detection/classification papers.
- Two claims from initial search synthesis could not be traced to a specific citable primary source and are therefore omitted from Cited Findings above rather than reported unsourced: (1) that Focal Loss is commonly used in waste-classification/detection work to counteract class-frequency imbalance, and (2) that standard geometric/photometric augmentation (flips, rotation, contrast normalization) is recommended as a baseline for occlusion/distortion robustness in waste pipelines. Both are very plausible (they are standard practice in general object detection) but should not be cited to a specific waste-domain paper without further verification.
- Image-infill for occlusion recovery was mentioned in the same search context as the SortWaste dataset but could not be confirmed as a claim made by the SortWaste paper itself versus a related discussion; treat as unconfirmed and requiring a direct-source check.
- Quantitative before/after mAP or accuracy deltas specifically isolating each augmentation strategy's individual contribution (e.g., "+X mAP from Focal Loss alone" in a waste-detection context) were not found within the tool-call budget for this research pass.
