# Monocular Depth for Mass/Volume Proxying and Economic/Value-Weighted Sorting Policies

## Which monocular depth models support metric depth output suitable for estimating object height/volume from a fixed overhead camera, and what accuracy do they report?

### Takeaway
Four zero-shot metric monocular depth models are actively used/benchmarked as of 2024-2025 — ZoeDepth, UniDepth (V1/V2), Depth Anything V2 (metric variant), and Apple's Depth Pro — and a cross-paper comparison table (from the Depth Pro paper) shows no single model dominates across all domains: UniDepth is strongest indoors (Sun-RGBD), Metric3D v2 is strongest on some outdoor sets, and Depth Pro ranks best on average across the mixed benchmark suite. For a fixed overhead camera on a conveyor (an indoor, close-range, roughly-known-geometry setting), UniDepth's indoor numbers (δ1 = 98.4% on NYU-Depth v2) and Depth Anything V2's reported ~0.074 AbsRel on outdoor/driving-style scenes suggest metric accuracy is very likely sufficient for coarse object-height/volume proxying, though none of these benchmarks are conveyor/bin-picking-specific.

### Cited Findings
- **Depth Pro (Apple, arXiv 2410.02073)**: zero-shot metric monocular depth model producing a 2.25-megapixel metric depth map in 0.3s on a V100 GPU (341.3ms at HD resolution); multi-scale ViT architecture with a DPT decoder, fixed 1536×1536 input, 504M parameters; predicts absolute-scale depth without requiring camera intrinsics metadata, and also does state-of-the-art focal length estimation from a single image. — [Depth Pro arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02073); [Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/depth-pro)
- **Cross-model δ1 comparison table (zero-shot, reported in the Depth Pro paper)**:
  | Dataset | Depth Pro | Depth Anything V2 | ZoeDepth | UniDepth | Metric3D v2 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Sun-RGBD (indoor) | 89.0 | 72.4 | 85.7 | **95.8** | 75.6 |
  | ETH3D | 41.5 | 36.3 | 34.2 | 25.3 | **87.7** |
  | nuScenes (driving) | 49.1 | 17.7 | 28.1 | 83.6 | **82.6** |
  | Avg. rank (lower=better, 1–6 scale) | **2.5** | 5.8 | 5.3 | 4.2 | 3.7 |
  — [Depth Pro arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02073)
- On nuScenes, AbsRel (lower is better): Depth Pro 0.287, Metric3D v2 0.197, UniDepth 0.138 — i.e., UniDepth has the lowest (best) relative error on that specific outdoor set even though Depth Pro has the better average rank across all datasets combined. — [Depth Pro arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02073)
- Boundary/occlusion accuracy (F1, higher better): Depth Pro 0.409 (Sintel) vs. Metric3D v2 0.321, Depth Anything V2 0.228, Marigold 0.068 — Depth Pro's key claimed advantage is sharp object-boundary tracing, not raw metric accuracy. — [Depth Pro arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.02073)
- **UniDepth (CVPR 2024, arXiv 2403.18913)**: self-promptable camera module (predicts dense camera representation without needing intrinsics) plus a pseudo-spherical output representation (θ azimuth, φ elevation, z_log depth) that disentangles camera and depth signals to avoid gradient entanglement. Trained on ~3M images (smaller than the 8–17M used by competitors) yet surpasses them in zero-shot generalization. — [UniDepth arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.18913v1); [CVPR page](https://cvpr.thecvf.com/virtual/2024/poster/31417)
- UniDepth reported numbers: NYU-Depth v2 δ1 = 98.4%, AbsRel = 5.78% (vs. ZoeDepth 95.2%/7.70%, Metric3D 92.6%/9.38%); KITTI Eigen-split δ1 = 98.6%, AbsRel = 4.21% (vs. ZoeDepth 96.5%/5.76%, Metric3D 97.5%/5.33%). — [UniDepth arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.18913v1)
- UniDepth improves δ1 over Metric3D and ZeroDepth by 5.8% and 7.3% respectively on NYU, and by 1.1% and 9.4% respectively on KITTI. — [UniDepth arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18913)
- **UniDepthV2** (arXiv 2502.20110) is a simplified follow-up, evaluated across ten diverse datasets including KITTI and NYU-Depth v2, reported to surpass prior approaches in zero-shot generalization (specific numeric table not retrieved in this research pass). — [UniDepthV2 arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20110)
- **Depth Anything V2 (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2406.09414)**: replaces labeled real training images with synthetic images, scales up the teacher model, and distills to student models via large-scale pseudo-labeled real images; releases six metric-depth checkpoints (three scales × indoor/outdoor domain-specific fine-tuning). Reported >10x faster inference than diffusion-based depth approaches (e.g., Marigold), with scales from 25M to 1.3B parameters. — [Depth Anything V2 arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09414); [project page](https://depth-anything-v2.github.io/)
- Depth Anything V2 (ViT-L) reported AbsRel ≈ 0.074 on KITTI (outdoor) and ≈ 0.135 on ScanNet (indoor) in third-party comparison summaries — note these figures come from aggregator/derivative sources rather than being independently confirmed by this research pass against the original paper's results tables (the official arXiv HTML did not surface a results table during fetch). — [Emergent Mind summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/depth-anything-v2-metric-outdoor); primary paper: [arXiv 2406.09414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09414)
- **ZoeDepth (arXiv 2302.12288)**: combines relative depth pretraining (12 datasets) with metric fine-tuning (2 datasets) via a metric bins module; flagship variant is ZoeD-M12-NK. Reported as the fastest of the compared models at 0.17s/image, but with "significant degradation in natural outdoor environments" relative to newer models. — [ZoeDepth arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12288); [HF blog comparison](https://huggingface.co/blog/Isayoften/monocular-depth-estimation-guide)
- A 2025 wildlife-monitoring benchmark (not conveyor/industrial, but a controlled real-world outdoor metric-depth test) found Depth Anything V2 gave the best overall balance of accuracy and speed: MAE 0.454 m, correlation 0.962, at 0.22s/image. — [Benchmark on Monocular Metric Depth Estimation in Wildlife Setting, arXiv 2510.04723](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04723v1)

### Inferences
- For a fixed, roughly-overhead conveyor camera (indoor, controlled lighting, known approximate working distance), UniDepth's indoor numbers (δ1 98.4%, AbsRel 5.78% on NYU) are the most directly relevant precedent found, since NYU-Depth v2 is an indoor, close/mid-range RGB-D benchmark closer in character to a conveyor rig than nuScenes/ETH3D driving-style outdoor benchmarks.
- None of the four models were benchmarked on a conveyor-belt, bin-picking, or produce-grading dataset specifically in the sources found — all accuracy numbers are transferred from general-purpose indoor/outdoor/driving benchmarks (NYU, KITTI, Sun-RGBD, ETH3D, nuScenes, Sintel, Spring). A project using these models for object-height/volume proxying should expect to validate/calibrate against ground-truth measurements on its own rig rather than relying on these published numbers directly.
- Because relative object height (not absolute scene depth) is what matters for volume-from-height proxying on a conveyor, sub-centimeter absolute metric error may matter less than boundary/edge sharpness (where Depth Pro claims an advantage) — this is a plausible design consideration but was not directly tested in any source found.

### Gaps
- No source in this pass reported an official, first-party results table with exact AbsRel/δ1 numbers for Depth Anything V2's metric checkpoints on standard benchmarks (NYU/KITTI) — the ViT-L KITTI ≈0.074 / ScanNet ≈0.135 AbsRel figures come from secondary/aggregator pages, not confirmed against the paper's own tables during this session (the arXiv HTML fetch did not surface the tables).
- No source addressed metric depth accuracy specifically at close range (~0.3–1.5 m, typical for an overhead conveyor rig) — all cited benchmarks are room-scale (NYU) or outdoor/driving-scale (KITTI, nuScenes, ETH3D), which may not transfer to a tight overhead camera setup.
- No source benchmarked these models specifically for volume/mass estimation error (as opposed to point-wise depth error), so the propagation from per-pixel depth accuracy to volume-estimation accuracy is not directly evidenced here (see next section for closest analogues).

## Has depth estimation been combined with RGB detection to estimate object volume or mass in sorting, recycling, bin-picking, or produce-grading applications? What pipelines and results?

### Takeaway
Depth+RGB (or RGB-only with a calibrated reference) fusion for volume/mass estimation is well established in **agricultural grading** (oranges, garlic, cassava, mango, apple), with reported R² in the 0.79–0.97 range depending on crop and method, but the research found in this pass shows **no published pipeline that estimates object volume or mass from monocular/RGB-D depth specifically within a recycling or waste-sorting system** — in that domain, RGB-D/depth is used instead for grasp-priority and occlusion/clutter reasoning (identifying the topmost graspable object), not for mass/volume proxying.

### Cited Findings
- **Oranges (2025, PMC12565508)**: RGB-only pipeline (no depth camera) — a consumer-grade Samsung Slim Fit Cam positioned 200mm above the fruit captures paired top/side 2D images; a 30mm×30mm calibrated physical marker converts pixel measurements to millimeters. 25 engineered geometric features (ellipsoid volume proxies, Ramanujan perimeter, Knud-Thomsen surface area, sphericity, aspect ratios) feed a Stacking Regressor (Gradient Boosting + XGBoost + LightGBM base learners, Lasso meta-learner). On 150 oranges (58–288 mL range): **R² = 0.971, MAE = 10.22 mL**, beating geometric-proxy baselines (R² = 0.920). Mass/density is obtained by integrating conveyor load cells (physical sensor, not vision) rather than vision-based mass estimation. Real-time pipeline runs at 15–25 fps (40–67ms/fruit), compatible with industrial conveyor speeds. — [Non-Destructive Volume Estimation of Oranges, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12565508/)
- **Garlic cloves (2025, ScienceDirect S0925521425001383)**: uses an Intel RealSense D405 **depth camera** capturing paired 2D (640×480 JPG) and 3D (PLY point cloud) images. Volume/mass predicted from length/width/height/mass features using SVM, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, kNN. Best results: **SVM for volume (R² = 0.786, MAPE = 0.084)**; **Random Forest for mass (R² = 0.849, MAPE = 0.098)**. Ellipsoid-volume and power-equation corrections were needed because garlic cloves are irregular/non-axially-symmetric, and pure 2D imaging was explicitly noted as inadequate for their shape. Stated application: automated garlic sorting/grading. — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925521425001383) (abstract-level access only; full text paywalled, not independently verified beyond the abstract/search-snippet level)
- **Cassava**: volume estimation using a consumer-grade RGB-D camera is reported in the literature (title/topic level only — full methodology and accuracy not retrieved in this pass). — [Volume estimation of cassava using consumer-grade RGB-D camera](https://www.academia.edu/50950840/Volume_estimation_of_cassava_using_consumer_grade_RGB_D_camera)
- **Mango**: a vision-based volume estimation method for automatic mango grading exists in the literature (ScienceDirect) — title/topic level only in this pass, full pipeline/results not retrieved. — [ScienceDirect S1537511020302403](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1537511020302403)
- **Apples**: 3D machine-vision system for apple fruit size estimation exists in the literature — title/topic level only, not independently fetched in this pass. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/345643825_Apple_fruit_size_estimation_using_a_3D_machine_vision_system)
- **General fruit/produce**: volumetric 3D reconstruction via multi-camera silhouette/voxel-carving methods is used for grading; RGB-D depth data matched to RGB images plus the thin-lens formula is cited as one general approach to computing fruit linear dimensions. — [Volumetric estimation using 3D reconstruction, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11042-018-6271-3)
- **Bin-picking (industrial parts, not waste)**: search-engine synthesis (not a directly fetched paper) described a depth-independent CNN co-learning category-agnostic instance segmentation, grasp-confidence scoring, and monocular depth estimation, with depth used for collision detection in grasp-pose prediction and transforming planar grasp poses into 3D world coordinates — i.e., depth for grasp geometry/collision-checking, not for volume/mass. A related claim noted monocular depth error worsening as object size decreases, conflicting with bin-picking's need for accurate small-object localization. **No primary paper could be identified or fetched behind this synthesis in this pass — treat as directionally indicative of the field, not a verified citation.**
- **XYZ-IBD dataset**: a high-precision bin-picking dataset for 6D pose estimation and monocular depth estimation — 15 industrial parts, 75 scenes, 22K images, millimeter-accurate annotations — indicates the field has benchmark infrastructure for monocular depth in bin-picking, but this is a pose/geometry benchmark, not a volume/mass benchmark. — [XYZ-IBD](https://xyz-ibd.github.io/)
- **Robotic waste sorting with RGB-D (KUKA LBR iiwa, 2025, MDPI Robotics 15(5):100)**: integrates a Vision-Language-Action model (Gemini VLA) with an RGB-D camera on a KUKA LBR iiwa cobot for waste sorting, explicitly without task-specific training/dataset collection. Per the paper's findings (retrieved via search synthesis; direct full-text fetch was blocked by the publisher, 403), **RGB-D sensing (specifically the depth channel) is used to identify the topmost object in clutter, establish grasp priority ordering, and distinguish graspable vs. non-graspable items** — confirmed by the authors as "indispensable for waste sorting in three-dimensional settings." This is depth-for-grasp-planning/occlusion-reasoning, not depth-for-volume/mass estimation. Contributions also include prompt-engineering strategies for waste identification and clutter-adapted grasping strategies (single-API-call picking for uncluttered scenes vs. different strategies for cluttered scenes). — [DOI: 10.3390/robotics15050100](https://doi.org/10.3390/robotics15050100) / [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2218-6581/15/5/100) (findings via search synthesis, not direct full-text fetch — publisher blocked automated access)
- **Waste/C&D (construction & demolition) RGB-D classification**: RGB-D fusion models built on Mask R-CNN segment object contours while classifying waste type; depth is added as a fourth channel to RGB, giving only a modest accuracy gain (+1.92–2.27 percentage points) over RGB-only in one cited model, with depth's main claimed benefit being robustness to dust/lighting/color variation rather than accuracy gain per se. — [RGB-D fusion models for construction and demolition waste detection, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0956053X21006668) (abstract-level access; full methodology not independently verified). MJU-Waste dataset (RGB + co-registered depth for indoor trash) is one benchmark used in this space.
- **Adjacent non-waste example of pipeline depth**: a heterogeneous-camera fusion method explicitly targets "material volume" detection by combining target detection with depth estimation (title strongly matches the query), but this paper's full text returned a 403 (paywalled/access-blocked) during this research pass, so its pipeline and results could not be verified beyond the title. — [AIP Advances, Detect material volume by fusing heterogeneous camera target detection and depth estimation information](https://pubs.aip.org/aip/adv/article/15/1/015132/3331970/Detect-material-volume-by-fusing-heterogeneous) (title-only; content inaccessible, treat as an unverified lead)

### Inferences
- The dominant pattern in agriculture is: RGB-only + a calibrated physical reference marker can reach very high volume-estimation accuracy (R² ≈ 0.97 for near-ellipsoidal produce like oranges) when the object's geometry is regular; a true depth camera becomes more valuable (and is explicitly justified by the authors) when object geometry is irregular/non-axially-symmetric (e.g., garlic cloves, R² ≈ 0.79–0.85, notably lower than the orange result). This suggests a waste-stream project (mixed, irregular object shapes) should expect volume-estimation accuracy closer to the garlic-clove regime (R² ~0.8) than the orange regime (R² ~0.97) unless objects are regularized (e.g., flattened cardboard, cylindrical cans) — this is an inference, not a directly cited cross-domain claim.
- In the one waste-specific RGB-D paper found (KUKA/VLA), depth is being used for a fundamentally different purpose (clutter/grasp-order reasoning) than what the user's project would need (volume/mass proxying for value estimation) — this is a genuine, evidenced gap: no source in this pass shows depth being used for mass/volume/value proxying specifically in a waste/recycling pipeline. A project doing this would be combining two currently-separate literatures (produce-grading volumetrics + waste-sorting RGB-D grasping).
- Mass estimation from vision alone (without a load cell) was not demonstrated with strong accuracy in any source found — the orange paper's "mass" figure actually comes from a physical load cell integrated into the conveyor, not from vision. This suggests pure vision-to-mass estimation (without density assumptions or a physical scale) remains a harder, less-validated problem than vision-to-volume.

### Gaps
- Could not access full text of the garlic-clove paper (ScienceDirect paywall) or the AIP Advances "material volume fusing" paper (403 error) — both are plausible close analogues but unverified beyond abstract/title level.
- Could not directly verify the MDPI KUKA LBR iiwa paper's full methodology (automated fetch blocked by publisher); findings above rely on search-engine-synthesized snippets rather than the primary text, and should be treated as lower-confidence than directly-fetched sources.
- No source found that combines a *metric* monocular depth foundation model (Depth Anything V2 metric, Depth Pro, UniDepth) specifically with volume/mass estimation for produce grading or waste sorting — the agricultural volume-estimation papers found use either RGB+calibration-marker or a consumer RGB-D sensor (RealSense), not the newer 2023-2025 metric depth foundation models. This is a concrete gap a student project could fill: no evidence any of Depth Anything V2/Depth Pro/UniDepth have been applied to object volume/mass proxying in sorting or grading contexts.
- No density/material-lookup approach (volume × assumed density-by-class = estimated mass, without a physical scale) was found evidenced in any sorting/recycling paper in this pass — this specific technique (which seems highly relevant to the student's likely approach) appears to be an unaddressed gap in the literature found.

## In waste/recycling economics, what are typical market values (price per kg or per ton) for recyclable materials as of recent years (2023-2026)?

### Takeaway
US curbside recyclable commodity prices are published monthly/regularly by *Resource Recycling* (national average curbside price index) and by regional bodies (NERC, CalRecycle); prices are volatile and **fell sharply through 2025** across nearly all grades (aluminum, PET, HDPE, OCC/cardboard, mixed paper), with a 2026 stabilization expected only as older processing capacity exits the market — so any "price per kg" used in a value-weighted metric should be treated as a live variable, not a constant, and the report/project should cite a specific snapshot date.

### Cited Findings
- **December 2025 snapshot (NERC, year-end 2025 report)**:
  - Aluminum cans: **78.75 ¢/lb** (down 9.3% over the year)
  - Steel cans: **$158.75/ton** (down 8.7%)
  - Natural HDPE: **46.81 ¢/lb** (down ~1/3 over the year)
  - Color HDPE: **2.81 ¢/lb** (down 48%)
  - PET beverage bottles: **5.38 ¢/lb** (down ~2/3 over the year — the steepest decline of any tracked grade)
  - Polypropylene (PP): **5.38 ¢/lb** (down 40%)
  - OCC (old corrugated containers / cardboard): **$46.88/ton** (down ~1/3 from the 2024 peak)
  - Residential Mixed Paper: **$20.31/ton** (down 50% from the 2024 peak)
  - Glass, clear bottles: **$38.56/ton**; brown: **$27.19/ton**; green: **$10.31/ton**; **mixed glass: –$25.31/ton (a negative price — processors pay a tipping fee to accept it)**
  - Narrative: 2025 was characterized as "a beating" for recycling markets across the board, driven by a K-shaped economic recovery suppressing packaging demand and paper-mill closures; modest stabilization is projected for 2026 as older capacity exits. — [NERC, "2025 Recycling Markets Took a Beating in a Weak Retail Year"](https://www.nerc.org/2025-recycling-markets-took-a-beating-in-a-weak-retail-year)

### Inferences
- Aluminum is consistently the highest-value-per-kg curbside material among the common grades tracked (tens of cents to ~$0.80–0.90/lb, i.e., roughly $1,700–2,000+/ton), cardboard/OCC and mixed paper are low-value-per-kg but high-volume, and mixed glass can carry a **negative** value (a cost, not a revenue) — meaning a genuinely economic value-weighted sorting policy should be able to represent negative-value/cost items, not just a ranked-positive-value list. This is an important design implication for the "value-weighted sorting" metric the user is considering, though it is an inference from the price data rather than a directly cited design recommendation.
- Because prices are this volatile (e.g., PET fell ~65% and natural HDPE ~33% within 2025 alone per the NERC report), any economic-value-weighted throughput metric proposed in a student project should either (a) explicitly parameterize price as a live/configurable input rather than a hardcoded constant, or (b) clearly caveat that reported $-values are a snapshot, to remain defensible/reproducible.

### Gaps
- No unified, single authoritative "price index" source (e.g., a single ISRI/ReMA numeric table) was retrieved directly — figures come from trade-press secondary reporting (Resource Recycling, NERC), which is standard practice in this industry (these outlets are themselves widely cited as the reference for curbside commodity pricing) but is worth flagging as not a primary-source exchange/index in the way e.g. LME is for metals.
- No global/international price series beyond the single Q1 2025 US/Germany/India HDPE snapshot was retrieved; the recycling economics figures found are overwhelmingly US-centric (Resource Recycling and NERC both report US curbside/MRF pricing).
- 2026 prices were not directly found (only a qualitative "modest stabilization expected" projection from the NERC report published referencing year-end 2025 data) — no confirmed 2026 numeric data point exists in the sources gathered.

## Does any published paper define or optimize a throughput/reward metric that weights sorting decisions by economic value of the material, rather than purely accuracy/mAP-based metrics?

### Takeaway
**No source found in this research pass — including Google's large-scale real-world RSS 2023 deployment, the delta-robot scrap-sorting RL paper, the circular-economy RL frameworks, and a title-level match on "value of information in sorting" that turned out to mean something else — defines or optimizes a "value-weighted sorting throughput" metric (i.e., $-value-recovered-per-hour or price-per-kg-weighted pick-priority) for a robotic sorting/recycling system at the individual-pick-decision level.** Economic/price data is used in the literature found, but only at the *facility-configuration* level (an MRF optimization patent that feeds daily material prices into a feed-rate/staffing optimizer to maximize net revenue over a shift) — never inside a per-item, vision-driven pick-selection policy. The closest robotics-level analogues weight by something *other* than market price: an MDP-style throughput objective (count/rate, not $), and "criticality"-weighted circularity metrics (CIRO7.2, CiRL) that use a thermodynamic/scarcity coefficient rather than market price. Given this — a real gap was searched for directly across robotics, RL-reward-design, circular-economy, and OR/MRF-economics literature, and consistently not found at the per-decision level — the specific metric the user is considering (an explicit $/kg- or $/hour-weighted sorting throughput/reward, applied to real-time vision-based pick decisions) is a genuine novel-contribution opportunity for a student project, not a rediscovery.

### Cited Findings
- **Deep RL at Scale: Sorting Waste in Office Buildings with a Fleet of Mobile Manipulators (Google, RSS 2023 — confirmed venue/year: Robotics: Science and Systems, Daegu, July 2023)**: a fleet of 23 RL-enabled mobile-manipulator robots deployed over 24 months across three Google office buildings, accumulating 9,527 hours of real-world robotic experience, combining scalable real-world deep RL with sim-to-real bootstrapping and auxiliary inputs from existing CV systems. **Reward is assigned per-episode when the robot picks up a misplaced object, moves it to the correct bin, and terminates the episode** (correctness/bin-placement based) — no economic, material-value, or contamination-cost weighting is described in this reward formulation. Validated with 4,800 evaluation trials across 240 waste-station configurations. This is the largest, most directly-relevant real-world deployment found in this research pass, and it **confirms the count/correctness-based (not value-weighted) reward pattern** at Google/industrial scale. — [arXiv 2305.03270](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03270); [Google Research blog](https://blog.research.google/2023/04/robotic-deep-rl-at-scale-sorting-waste.html); [project page](https://rl-at-scale.github.io/)
- **Reinforcement Learning to improve delta robot throws for sorting scrap metal (arXiv 2406.13453)**: uses TD3/SAC/PPO to learn "pick-and-throw" release position/speed for delta robots sorting scrap metal; reports **89% accuracy while increasing throughput by 51% in simulation**, with sim-to-real transfer via domain randomization and no real-world fine-tuning needed. The reward function's exact formulation (accuracy-only vs. any value-weighting) could not be confirmed from the abstract/HTML fetch in this pass — the full reward equations are in the PDF body which exceeded this session's fetch size/parsing limits. Throughput here is a **count/rate metric (items sorted per unit time), not a $-value-weighted metric**, based on how it's described in the abstract. — [arXiv 2406.13453](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.13453)
- **Materials Recovery Facility (MRF) process optimization exists at the facility/line-configuration level, using live material prices as an input** — a patented approach ("Materials recovery facility process optimization via unit operation feedback") describes a computer program into which **daily material prices are entered along with manual-sorting hourly costs and required processing volume**, computing the optimal processing feed-rate and staffing level to **maximize net revenue**, trading off throughput/feed-rate against output purity/quality. This is genuine price-informed economic optimization — but it operates at the level of *facility configuration* (feed rate, personnel allocation) over a shift/day, not at the level of a *single vision-based pick decision* on an individual detected item. — [USPTO patent 7994448](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7994448); related: [USPTO patent 7893378](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7893378)

Full text was blocked by the publisher (403) in this pass, so methodology is confirmed only at the abstract/title level. — [ScienceDirect S0921344917304123](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0921344917304123) (title/abstract-level only; full text inaccessible)
- **CIRO7.2 (arXiv 2506.11748)**: models a "thermodynamical material network" with a reinforcement-learning-controlled robotic disassembler, optimizing a **circularity metric (λ)** based on "compartmental dynamical thermodynamics" and material **criticality coefficients** (e.g., 0.1 vs. 0.95 for two materials in their test case) — i.e., decisions are weighted by a criticality/scarcity coefficient, not a market price. Reported circularity results range from λ = −2.1 (best case, disassembling 2×1kg parts) to λ = −7.2 (worst case, 4×1kg parts + 3kg chassis). This is conceptually adjacent to (but distinct from) a $-value-weighted metric — it weights by material criticality/scarcity rather than market price per kg. — [arXiv 2506.11748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11748)
- **CiRL (arXiv 2505.21536)**: an open-source library of RL environments for "circularity control" of solid/fluid materials in supply-recovery chains, built on the same thermodynamical-network framework and Stable-Baselines3 — presented as generalizing CIRO7.2's approach to more environments. Individual environment reward functions were not retrieved (abstract-only access in this pass), so it is unconfirmed whether any included environment uses market-price weighting specifically. — [arXiv 2505.21536](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21536)

### Inferences
- Based on the sources located, the literature draws a fairly clean line between (a) **accuracy/count-based objectives** (mAP, sorting correctness, throughput-as-items/hour) used in nearly all robotics/CV sorting papers found — now including the Google RSS 2023 deployment, the largest real-world example located — and (b) **aggregate or facility-level business-case economics** (ROI, payback period, market size, MRF feed-rate/staffing revenue optimization) used in techno-economic/industry/OR reports — with essentially nothing found bridging the two at the level of a *per-decision, price-weighted objective function* (e.g., reward = Σ price_i × P(correctly sort item i) − cost of a miss) inside an actual real-time, vision-driven sorting-policy optimization.
- The "criticality"-weighted circularity metric in CIRO7.2/CiRL is the closest conceptual relative found in robotics/RL specifically — it establishes that *some* prior work weights disassembly/sorting decisions by a non-uniform per-material coefficient — but criticality (material scarcity/strategic importance) is explicitly a different axis from market price per kg, and neither paper appears (based on abstracts) to use live/market economic data.
- The MRF process-optimization patent shows the *facility-operations* field already treats material price as a first-class optimization input — so the underlying idea "let material price drive an operating decision" is not new to the waste industry in general. What appears genuinely absent is that same idea pushed down to the *individual, vision-based pick decision* (i.e., a per-item, real-time policy rather than a shift-level feed-rate/staffing dial). That is a meaningful, fairly precise scope for the novelty claim.
- Both of the most promising candidate prior-art papers found in this pass — the tandfonline "value of information" look-ahead paper and the Google RSS 2023 large-scale deployment — were checked directly for their precise meaning of "value"/reward, and neither weights by material economic value: the former means look-ahead information value, the latter uses a correctness/bin-placement reward. This closes out the two strongest counter-examples that could have undermined the novelty conclusion.

## Are there simulation or queueing-theory approaches to modeling a sorting robot/actuator under a throughput constraint choosing which detected items to act on?

### Takeaway
Classical queueing-theory treatments of robotic pick-and-place on conveyor belts exist and go back decades (e.g., 2D Poisson-process and M/G/1-with-impatience models of part arrival vs. robot pick rate), and knapsack-style formulations are used for resource-constrained item/task selection in adjacent warehouse-robotics contexts — but no source found in this pass combines queueing/throughput-constraint modeling specifically with an economic-value objective for choosing which detected items to act on; existing throughput-constrained selection work optimizes for pick-rate/gripping-rate/count, not $-value.

### Cited Findings
- Classic queueing-theoretic treatment (part-flow on conveyor belts, pick-and-place performance): "Sorting of items on a moving conveyor belt. Part 2: performance evaluation and optimization of pick-and-place operations" models the sorting-robot problem in terms of throughput and grip-rate optimization strategies for the order in which items are gripped off a moving belt. — [ScienceDirect S0736584599000411](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0736584599000411) / [ResearchGate mirror](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/245190308_Sorting_of_items_on_a_moving_conveyor_belt_Part_2_Performance_evaluation_and_optimization_of_pick-and-place_operations)
- "Estimating and optimizing throughput of a robotic part feeder using queueing theory" (IEEE) and its follow-up "Optimizing robotic part feeder throughput with queueing theory" directly apply queueing models to robotic feeder throughput optimization. — [IEEE Xplore document 727298](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/727298/); [Ingenta Connect follow-up](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/content/mcb/033/2007/00000027/00000002/art00006)
- For robotic part feeders on conveyors, two specific queueing models are used depending on feed mode: a **2D Poisson process** for intermittent-motion feeding, and an **M/G/1 queue with customer impatience** for continuous-motion feeding (where an item that passes the robot's reach zone unpicked is analogous to a "customer" leaving/reneging the queue). — [IEEE Xplore, Estimating and optimizing throughput of a robotic part feeder using queueing theory](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/727298/); [Ingenta Connect follow-up](https://www.ingentaconnect.com/content/mcb/033/2007/00000027/00000002/art00006) (search-engine-summarized from these two sources; full equations not independently verified from primary text in this pass)
- Key performance parameters defined in this literature: **gripping rate** (fraction of items successfully picked out of total presented) and **efficiency** (actual throughput ÷ maximum theoretical throughput, in items/time). System failure modes are explicitly named: **starvation** (no items visible/available to pick) and **saturation** (too many items causing occlusion/pose-identification/grasp failure). — [ScienceDirect, Sorting of items on a moving conveyor belt. Part 2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0736584599000411) (search-engine-summarized; full text not independently verified from primary source in this pass)
- "Queuing theory based part-flow estimation in a pick-and-place task with a multi-robot system" extends this to multi-robot conveyor sorting cells. — [ResearchGate 325769948](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325769948_Queuing_theory_based_part-flow_estimation_in_a_pick-and-place_task_with_a_multi-robot_system)
- "Development of the algorithm for the optimization of objects pick up sequence on a conveyor belt" addresses the specific sub-problem of choosing pick *order/sequence* under a moving-belt/reach-window constraint. — [ResearchGate 347345043](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347345043_Development_of_the_algorithm_for_the_optimization_of_objects_pick_up_sequence_on_a_conveyor_belt)
- Knapsack-problem framings are used in adjacent robotic/warehouse scheduling contexts: e.g., "multiple knapsack problem for racking selection," and general task-scheduling-as-knapsack framings where "the knapsack represents the allocable time span... and the items to pack are the observations with individual observing times" (this specific citation is from an astronomy-scheduling context but is the clearest general statement of the knapsack-for-time-constrained-task-selection analogy found). — [ResearchGate 332847875](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332847875_Multiple_knapsack_problem_for_racking_selection_model); [AIP scheduling-as-optimization](https://www.aip.de/en/research/robotics/scheduling-as-an-optimisation-problem/)
- Robotic Mobile Fulfillment System (warehouse order-picking) research jointly optimizes pod selection, robot scheduling, and station assignment via Mixed Integer Programs and heuristics (dynamic programming, simulated annealing) — a structurally similar resource-constrained-selection problem to conveyor sorting, but for order-fulfillment (picking specific SKUs to fulfill orders) rather than waste/material sorting. This characterization comes from search-engine synthesis across multiple RMFS scheduling papers rather than one directly fetched primary source, so it should be treated as a general field description rather than a specific paper's finding. A related, directly-titled paper on real-time storage allocation in RMFS via supervised learning and tree search exists in this literature but was not fetched for methodology detail in this pass. — [arXiv 2106.02450](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.02450) (title-level reference only; not the source of the MIP/pod-selection/station-assignment characterization above)
- Priority-dispatching rules from a related transport/pick-robot task-scheduling comparative study: **STD** (shortest time difference between transport-pick robot pairing), **SPT** (shortest processing time first), **LPT** (longest processing time first) — classic OR dispatching heuristics applied to robot task scheduling, none of which are inherently value-weighted (they optimize for time/makespan, not $-value) but could in principle be adapted (e.g., a "weighted SPT" analogous to WSJF). — [Springer, Task scheduling for transport and pick robots in logistics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43684-021-00017-9)
- Task-prioritization for warehouse item picking has been shown to use **prior probability of successful pick** (from vision/grasp confidence) to reorder the pick queue — i.e., picking success-likelihood is used as a priority weight, which is structurally similar to (but not the same as) weighting by economic value; a value-weighted version would multiply this success probability by price rather than using it alone. — [ResearchGate 321821478, Task prioritization for automated robotic item picking](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321821478_Task_prioritization_for_automated_robotic_item_picking)

### Inferences
- The mathematical machinery needed for a value-weighted, throughput-constrained sorting policy already exists piecewise in the literature: (1) queueing models (M/G/1 with impatience, 2D Poisson arrivals) characterize the arrival/service-rate constraint; (2) knapsack/MDP formulations characterize constrained item-selection under a resource budget; (3) grasp-confidence-weighted prioritization characterizes using a per-item scalar weight to reorder a pick queue. Combining (3)'s "weight per item" with a market $/kg value instead of (or multiplied by) a success probability, inside a (1)/(2)-style throughput-constrained selection model, is a coherent and technically well-grounded way to construct the "value-weighted sorting throughput" metric the user is considering — but this exact combination was not found already published in this research pass, reinforcing the novelty conclusion from the previous section.
- The "starvation vs. saturation" framing from the classic conveyor-feeder queueing literature is directly relevant to a picks-per-second-constrained project: it gives a vocabulary/model for what happens when detected-item arrival rate exceeds actuator throughput (saturation → the system must choose which items to act on, which is exactly where a value-weighting policy would be inserted as the tie-breaking/selection rule).

- No source found applies queueing theory or knapsack/MDP scheduling specifically to a *recycling or waste* conveyor sorting robot — all the queueing-theory and knapsack sources found are generic industrial part-feeding, warehouse order-fulfillment, or (in one case) astronomy-scheduling contexts. The specific combination of {conveyor queueing theory} + {waste/recycling materials} + {economic value weighting} was not found anywhere in one place.
- Full text of the core queueing-theory papers (the two "Sorting of items on a moving conveyor belt" ScienceDirect/IEEE papers) was not retrieved in this pass (only abstracts/titles via search) — exact model equations, assumptions, and quantitative results were not verified firsthand and are described here only at the level the search snippets provided.
- No source was found giving a concrete numeric example of "picks per second" actuator constraints for a real waste-sorting robot (e.g., typical industrial waste-sorting delta-robot or pick-and-place-arm cycle times) to ground a simulation's throughput parameter — this is a factual gap a student project would need to fill from equipment datasheets (e.g., AMP Robotics, Tomra, ZenRobotics/Terex commercial systems) rather than from academic literature, and no such datasheet-level figure was retrieved in this research pass.
