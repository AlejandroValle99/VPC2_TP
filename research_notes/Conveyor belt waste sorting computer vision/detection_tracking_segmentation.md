# Object Detection, Tracking, and Segmentation Model Choices for Conveyor-Belt Waste/Material Sorting Pipelines

*Provenance key used throughout: "(primary text fetched)" means the cited page/PDF was directly retrieved and read in this research pass; "(search-summary only)" means the figure comes from a search-engine-generated synthesis of the source and was not independently confirmed by fetching the primary document — treat these with correspondingly lower confidence.*

## What YOLO-family/RT-DETR real-time detector variants have been used or benchmarked for waste/recyclables detection, with reported mAP, FPS, and latency?

### Takeaway
YOLOv5/v8/v11/v12 variants dominate published waste-detection work, with lightweight/pruned versions reaching mAP@0.5 in the 58–97% range and inference latency from ~10–40 ms on GPU/embedded hardware; RT-DETR variants are emerging as a competitive alternative, with one lightweight robotic-sorting variant reporting mAP@50 of 97%. Most waste-specific papers report accuracy metrics thoroughly but omit rigorous latency/FPS figures measured on stated hardware, so FPS numbers below mix waste-specific and adjacent-domain (general YOLO, agriculture, hygiene) sources where explicitly noted.

### Cited Findings
- YOLO-SAT (pruned, Self-Adversarial-Trained YOLOv12 variant) for desert waste detection/classification achieves mAP@0.5 up to 94.6% with 16.3 ms latency and a 4.67 MB model size, targeted at embedded/drone deployment — [YOLO-SAT (arXiv 2511.03888)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.03888) (search-summary only)
- EcoDetect-YOLO (built on YOLOv5s) for domestic waste exposure detection: mAP@0.5 = 58.1% vs. 53.4% for the YOLOv5s baseline (+4.7 pp); FPS = 39.36 vs. 41.37 for baseline (a 2.01 FPS decrease); model size 15.7 MB vs. 14.4 MB; params 7.0M vs 6.7M; FLOPs 20.0G vs 15.8G. Benchmarked on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU (24 GB card, paper states "40 GB" memory) with an Intel Xeon Silver 4210 CPU (2.20 GHz, 32 GB RAM), CUDA 11.8 — [EcoDetect-YOLO (PMC11280945)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11280945/) (primary text fetched)
- MRS-YOLO achieved a 3.6 percentage-point mAP@50 improvement over a YOLOv8 baseline, validated on 12,072 samples across 10 categories including recyclable metal and paper — [MRS-YOLO (PMC11244501)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11244501/) (search-summary only)
- A Scene-Guided YOLO framework (YOLOv8 baseline + dual attention for contextual fusion) achieved a 7.3 percentage-point improvement in mAP@0.5 over baseline YOLOv8, tested across five different real-world scene types for solid waste detection — [Scene-guided YOLO (Discover AI, Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44163-026-01749-4) (search-summary only)
- On the underwater waste detection task, YOLOv8 achieved mAP 0.809, precision 0.832, recall 0.727, when compared against YOLOv7, YOLOv9, YOLOv10 and Faster R-CNN; YOLOv8 reportedly outperformed the other variants on precision and mAP — [Underwater Waste Detection: YOLOv7–10 vs Faster R-CNN (arXiv 2507.18967)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.18967) (search-summary only; two direct-fetch attempts on the PDF and HTML version both failed — PDF parsed as unreadable binary, HTML returned 404 — so per-model FPS/latency and the full comparison table could not be verified beyond this abstract-level summary)
- RTDRNet-lite, a lightweight modified RT-DETR for robotic waste sorting, is reported to achieve mAP@50 of 97%, using lightweight C2f modules in the detection head to cut computational complexity "without dramatic change in accuracy" — [RTDRNet-lite (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0956053X25005756) (search-summary only; direct fetch blocked with 403 Forbidden on ScienceDirect and a CAPTCHA/cookie wall on the PubMed abstract page, so FPS/latency and hardware details could not be extracted at all)
- On the ZeroWaste-f benchmark (4,503 labeled images, 27,000 instances, 4 material categories, severe class imbalance — cardboard >66% of annotations, metal <2%), fine-tuned closed-set detectors scored: YOLO11(L) 34.0 mAP, RT-DETR(L) 35.1 mAP, DINO(Swin-L) 48.3 mAP, Co-DETR(Swin-L) and DETA(Swin-L) both 51.6 mAP. Training used a single NVIDIA A100 (40 GB) GPU; no FPS/latency figures were reported in this paper — [Robust and Label-Efficient Deep Waste Detection (arXiv 2508.18799)](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18799) (primary text fetched)
- In an adjacent domain (hygiene compliance monitoring, not waste, but a rigorous same-hardware nano-model comparison relevant to real-time deployment feasibility): on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (16 GB, Google Colab), YOLOv8n reached mAP@50 85.6% at 336.30 FPS; YOLOv10n reached mAP@50 85.7% at 381.15 FPS (best accuracy/speed trade-off); YOLOv12n reached mAP@50 80.8% at 277.89 FPS — [Benchmarking Lightweight YOLO Detectors (PMC12526732)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12526732/) (primary text fetched)
- Ultralytics states YOLO11 and YOLOv8 deliver "sub-15ms latencies" when exported to TensorRT on Jetson hardware (Jetson AGX Thor, Jetson AGX Orin, Jetson Orin Nano Super, Jetson Orin NX 16GB), with YOLO11n in FP16 mode showing 7–13 ms inference time on Jetson Orin Nano Super — [YOLO11 on NVIDIA Jetson (Ultralytics blog)](https://www.ultralytics.com/blog/ultralytics-yolo11-on-nvidia-jetson-orin-nano-super-fast-and-efficient) (page fetched directly, but the full per-variant benchmark table — FPS/latency per n/s/m/l/x size at each precision — was not present in the extracted text; only these summary claims were retrievable)
- Cross-domain sanity check (weed detection, agriculture, not waste): YOLOv11 inference time 13.5 ms (fastest), YOLOv10 19.3 ms, YOLOv8 23 ms; YOLOv9 had the highest accuracy at mAP@0.5 = 0.935 in that study — [Comparative YOLOv8/9/10/11 for weed detection (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772375524002533) (search-summary only)
- Mask-based segmentation alternative for waste/industrial sorting (not detection, but relevant as a speed baseline for the segmentation question too): a Mask R-CNN transfer-learning model for waste-bottle instance segmentation achieved 44 FPS on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU — [Instance Segmentation of Waste Bottles with Mask R-CNN (arXiv 2204.07437)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.07437) (search-summary only); an improved Mask R-CNN model for coal-gangue (industrial mineral) sorting reached 58 FPS at 81.7% accuracy — [Improved Mask R-CNN for coal gangue sorting (Taylor & Francis)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19392699.2025.2505448) (search-summary only); and an adaptive lightweight Mask R-CNN for underwater debris instance segmentation reached 30 FPS — [Adaptive lightweight Mask R-CNN for underwater debris (PMC13136368)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13136368/) (search-summary only)

### Inferences
- For a real-time, actuator-driving pipeline, nano/small YOLO variants (YOLOv8n/v10n/v11n or pruned custom variants like YOLO-SAT/EcoDetect-YOLO) are the practical choice: they consistently land in the 15–40 ms per-frame latency range on GPU hardware (RTX 3090, Tesla T4, Jetson-class edge devices), well within typical belt-sorting cycle budgets, whereas larger transformer-based closed-set detectors (DINO, Co-DETR, DETA) trade 15–20 mAP points of accuracy gain for unreported (likely much higher) latency, making them better suited to offline benchmarking/labeling than in-line control.
- RT-DETR variants (RT-DETR(L), RTDRNet-lite) show competitive or superior mAP to same-scale YOLO models on waste data, suggesting RT-DETR is a credible detector-family alternative to YOLO for a waste-sorting CV pipeline, but the evidence base for its real-time latency specifically on waste/recyclables is thinner and less independently verified than for YOLO (RTDRNet-lite's own FPS/latency figures could not be retrieved at all in this pass).
- Waste detection mAP figures vary hugely by dataset/domain (net domestic waste ~58%, desert/drone waste ~94.6%, underwater waste ~81%, ZeroWaste-f closed-set best ~51.6%), indicating that reported accuracy numbers are not directly comparable across papers and any architecture decision should be validated against the specific target material stream and camera conditions rather than transplanting a headline mAP number.

### Gaps
- No paper found gives a full detector comparison (mAP + FPS + latency, same hardware, same waste dataset) spanning YOLOv8 through YOLOv11/v12 AND RT-DETR simultaneously; the numbers above are stitched from several different studies/datasets/hardware setups, and most were only accessible via search-engine summary rather than direct primary-text confirmation (see provenance tags above).
- RTDRNet-lite's FPS/latency and the underwater YOLOv7-10 comparison's per-model FPS could not be retrieved (paywall/CAPTCHA/binary-PDF parsing failures) despite being among the most directly relevant sources found for those questions.
- No paper was found benchmarking detector accuracy/speed with the camera and lighting conditions specific to conveyor-belt sorting facilities (as opposed to static/drone/underwater imagery), aside from the closed-set numbers on ZeroWaste-f (which is drawn from real recycling-facility imagery but doesn't report speed).
- A generic, unqualified "YOLOv8 ≈260 FPS / YOLOv10 ≈280 FPS / YOLOv11 ≈290 FPS" figure surfaced during research (secondary aggregation of "official Ultralytics metrics," no stated model size or hardware); it was excluded from Cited Findings as unusable without those qualifiers. The properly hardware-qualified PMC12526732 table above should be used instead.

## What multi-object tracking algorithms (ByteTrack, BoT-SORT, DeepSORT, etc.) give persistent IDs to objects moving through a camera's field of view on a belt, and are there published results specific to conveyor belts/industrial sorting?

### Takeaway
ByteTrack and BoT-SORT are the trackers most commonly paired with YOLO detectors for belt/production-line object counting and identity persistence in practitioner writeups and at least one peer-reviewed agricultural inspection paper, but rigorous published tracking-accuracy metrics (MOTA/IDF1/HOTA) specific to conveyor belts or industrial sorting were not found — the only quantitative tracker benchmarks available are from general pedestrian/vehicle MOT leaderboards (MOT17/MOT20), not belt-specific datasets.

### Cited Findings
- ByteTrack achieves 80.3 MOTA, 77.3 IDF1, and 63.1 HOTA on the MOT17 test set (private detections) while running at 30 FPS on a single V100 GPU; it ranked 1st on the MOT17 leaderboard at publication and also achieved state-of-the-art results on MOT20, HiEve, and BDD100K — [ByteTrack (arXiv 2110.06864 / ECCV 2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06864) (search-summary only)
- BoT-SORT-ReID achieves 80.5 MOTA, 80.2 IDF1, 65.0 HOTA on MOT17 (first tracker to exceed 80 IDF1), and 77.8 MOTA, 77.5 IDF1, 63.3 HOTA on MOT20, ranking first on both test sets across all three main metrics — [BoT-SORT (arXiv 2206.14651)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.14651) (search-summary only)
- BoT-SORT's architectural improvement over SORT/DeepSORT-style trackers: it changes the Kalman filter state vector to directly estimate object width/height (instead of aspect ratio), and adds camera motion compensation (CMC) so that predicted object position does not lead/lag during non-uniform target motion — described as a good fit for "crowded scenes, moving cameras, and warehouse environments where occlusion is constant" — [BoT-SORT explainer (Labellerr)](https://www.labellerr.com/blog/bot-sort-tracking/) (search-summary only; practitioner blog, not peer-reviewed)
- DeepSORT, retrospectively evaluated with a strong YOLOX-X detector, achieves 60.3 MOTA, 61.2 IDF1, 48.8 HOTA on MOT17, substantially below ByteTrack/BoT-SORT-ReID on the same benchmark; ~52 FPS reported in one implementation. The source itself flags that HOTA did not exist when DeepSORT was originally published (2017), so this is a modern re-evaluation, not the original paper's own reported numbers — [DeepSORT/MOT comparison summary (Forasoft)](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/ai-for-video-engineering/articles-ai/multi-object-tracking-deepsort-bytetrack-ocsort) (search-summary only; practitioner article)
- A peer-reviewed two-stage detection-tracking framework for apple quality inspection on a dense conveyor belt uses an orchard-trained YOLOv8 model for localization, ByteTrack for multi-object tracking to maintain persistent identities, and a ResNet18 defect classifier; it introduces "track-level aggregation... to enforce temporal consistency and reduce prediction oscillation across frames," reporting "improved stability compared to frame-wise inference" — [Two-Stage Detection-Tracking for Apple Inspection (arXiv 2602.19278)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.19278) (primary text fetched — both the PDF and the abstract/HTML page were directly retrieved — but neither exposed numeric mAP, FPS, latency, belt speed, or MOTA/IDF1 tracking-accuracy figures in the extracted content; this is the single most directly on-topic paper found — YOLOv8+ByteTrack on a literal dense conveyor belt — but its quantitative results remain a gap in these notes despite direct-fetch attempts)
- ByteTrack combined with YOLOv8 is described in multiple practitioner sources as the standard approach for counting objects (boxes, packets, parcels) on automated conveyor belts, assigning a unique ID to each object as it crosses the frame (top to bottom) specifically to prevent double-counting — [Roboflow: What is ByteTrack?](https://blog.roboflow.com/what-is-bytetrack-computer-vision/); [Roboflow: Counting Objects on a Conveyor Belt](https://blog.roboflow.com/counting-objects-conveyor-belt/); [YOLOv8+ByteTrack conveyor counting (Medium)](https://medium.com/@aftab.ktk437/enhancing-object-counting-on-automated-conveyor-belts-with-yolov8-and-bytetrack-38819b2bb69b) (search-summary only; these are practitioner/blog sources, not peer-reviewed, but directly describe the belt use case)

### Inferences
- Given BoT-SORT-ReID's edge over ByteTrack on both MOT17 and MOT20 (higher MOTA/IDF1/HOTA) and its explicit design for occlusion-heavy, camera-motion scenes, it is architecturally the stronger candidate for a belt-sorting pipeline where items can partially occlude each other and vibration/camera jitter is possible — but this advantage is evidenced only on general pedestrian-tracking benchmarks, not on any belt/industrial dataset, so it should be treated as a plausible-but-unverified transfer rather than a proven result.
- DeepSORT's appearance-embedding re-identification step (which ByteTrack omits by default) is typically not necessary for a single-camera, single-pass conveyor belt where objects move in one direction and do not need long-term re-identification after leaving the frame; this likely explains why ByteTrack (simpler, faster, no ReID network) is the tracker of choice in the practitioner conveyor-counting examples found, since belt tracking is closer to short-lived "count and forget" tracking than long-horizon multi-camera re-identification.
- The near-total absence of published MOTA/IDF1 numbers on any belt-specific or industrial-sorting dataset is itself a notable finding: architecture justification for a belt-sorting pipeline will need to lean on (a) general MOT-benchmark numbers as a proxy for tracker quality, and (b) the qualitative apple-inspection precedent (YOLOv8+ByteTrack proven to "work" on a dense belt, per that paper's claims) rather than on any quantitative belt-specific accuracy figure.

### Gaps
- No MOTA/IDF1/HOTA (or any tracking-accuracy metric) specific to a conveyor belt, waste-sorting line, or industrial parts-sorting dataset was found anywhere in this search — this is a clear gap the report should flag explicitly.
- The apple-inspection paper (arXiv 2602.19278) is the closest primary source to "ByteTrack on a waste-adjacent conveyor belt," and its full text was directly fetched twice in this pass, but its quantitative results (accuracy, FPS, belt speed, MOTA/IDF1) still could not be extracted from the retrieved content — the PDF returned largely as unparsed binary and only the abstract text was legible. A further attempt with a dedicated PDF-text extraction tool (rather than WebFetch) would be needed to close this gap.
- No comparative study of BoT-SORT vs. ByteTrack vs. DeepSORT specifically on a belt/production-line video dataset was found; all comparative numbers above come from general pedestrian MOT benchmarks (MOT17/MOT20) via search-summary only (not independently fetched from the primary papers), which may not transfer cleanly to top-down or oblique belt camera geometry with rigid, non-articulated objects.

## Has SAM 2 or similar video segmentation models with native mask propagation/memory been used for combined instance segmentation + tracking of objects on a belt or similar video settings? What speed/accuracy tradeoffs are reported?

### Takeaway
No evidence was found of SAM2 being applied specifically to conveyor-belt or waste-sorting video; the technique is an active general-purpose research area (zero-shot video object segmentation + tracking) with published real-time-capable speeds on high-end GPUs (30+ FPS for base/small/tiny variants on an A100), but its application to belt-style industrial sorting appears to be an open opportunity rather than a demonstrated, published result.

### Cited Findings
- SAM 2 extends the original Segment Anything Model with a streaming memory bank that retains object-specific information across video frames, enabling video object segmentation and tracking (VOST) with "improved inference speed while maintaining competitive segmentation accuracy" relative to running SAM per-frame — [What is Segment Anything 2? (Roboflow)](https://blog.roboflow.com/what-is-segment-anything-2/); [SAM 2 paper (arXiv 2408.00714)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.00714) (search-summary only)
- SAM2 achieves real-time (30+ FPS) performance for all but the largest model variant. Reported per-variant speeds on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (PyTorch 2.3.1, CUDA 12.1, bfloat16): Hiera-T 47.2 FPS, Hiera-S 43.3 FPS, Hiera-B+ 43.8 FPS, Hiera-L 30.2 FPS — [SAM 2 docs (Ultralytics)](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/sam-2) (search-summary only)
- Seg2Track-SAM2 integrates a pre-trained (interchangeable) object detector with SAM2 plus a novel "Seg2Track" module to handle track initialization, management, and reinforcement; it requires no fine-tuning and is detector-agnostic, aimed at zero-shot-generalizable multi-object tracking and segmentation — [Seg2Track-SAM2 (arXiv 2509.11772)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.11772) (search-summary only)
- SAM2MOT proposes a "segmentation-driven" paradigm for multi-object tracking that departs from the conventional detect-then-associate framework, built on SAM2's video segmentation generalization — [SAM2MOT (arXiv 2504.04519)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.04519) (search-summary only)
- General web search results returned no paper or industry report describing SAM2 (or a SAM2-based MOTS framework) applied to conveyor-belt monitoring, waste sorting, or comparable industrial moving-object sorting scenarios.

### Inferences
- SAM2's per-frame speed on an A100 (30–47 FPS depending on variant) is in the same rough ballpark as YOLO+tracker pipelines, suggesting it is technically fast enough to be considered for a belt-sorting pipeline if pixel-accurate masks (rather than boxes) are valuable for the downstream actuator (e.g., precise air-jet targeting or gripper contact point selection) — but this inference is based on general-purpose VOS benchmarks, not any belt-relevant one, and A100-class hardware is a much heavier compute footprint than the edge/embedded GPUs (Jetson, RTX 3090, T4) seen in the waste-detector literature, which is a real deployment-cost consideration.
- The complete absence of belt/industrial-sorting SAM2 literature suggests this is a genuine research gap/opportunity rather than a solved-and-abandoned problem — a pipeline that combines a fast closed-set detector (for triggering/localization) with SAM2 for mask refinement only on candidate regions (rather than running SAM2 densely on every frame) is a plausible architecture not yet validated in published work, and any claim of "SAM2 works well for belt sorting" would currently be unsupported by direct evidence.

### Gaps
- No published benchmark of SAM2 (or SAM2MOT/Seg2Track-SAM2) accuracy or speed on any waste, recycling, or conveyor-belt dataset was found.
- No direct accuracy comparison (e.g., mask mAP or IoU) between SAM2-based tracking and a YOLO-seg/Mask R-CNN baseline on the same waste data was found; the Mask R-CNN FPS figures found (see Key Question 1: 30–58 FPS on waste-adjacent tasks) are the closest available proxy for segmentation-model speed in this domain but do not involve SAM2's memory-propagation mechanism.
- Latency of SAM2's memory-attention mechanism as sequence length grows (relevant for a belt running continuously for hours) was not addressed in any source found.
- A claimed "SAM2Plus" variant surfaced in search results with a reported speed of "13.13 FPS with the Large backbone," but no traceable source URL could be identified for this claim during this research pass; it is omitted from Cited Findings above and should not be treated as verified.

## Has open-vocabulary detection (YOLO-World, Grounding DINO, OWL-ViT) been applied to waste/recyclable material detection to handle novel material types without retraining? What accuracy/latency do these report versus closed-vocabulary detectors?

### Takeaway
Open-vocabulary detectors (YOLO-World, Grounding DINO, OWLv2) have been directly benchmarked against closed-vocabulary detectors on a real waste dataset (ZeroWaste-f) and substantially underperform on raw accuracy (roughly 6.5–13.5 mAP zero-shot vs. 34–54 mAP for fine-tuned closed-set models), with prompt engineering and semi-supervised pseudo-labeling closing much but not all of the gap; no latency/FPS figures for open-vocabulary detectors on waste data were found in any source. This is the best-evidenced key question in this research pass — all findings below come from one paper whose full text was directly fetched.

### Cited Findings
- On ZeroWaste-f, zero-shot open-vocabulary detection with simple class-name-only prompts scored: OWLv2 (ViT-L) 7.3 mAP, Grounding DINO (Swin-B) 7.0 mAP, YOLO-World ≈6.5 mAP (the lowest of the three) — [Robust and Label-Efficient Deep Waste Detection (arXiv 2508.18799)](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18799) (primary text fetched)
- Using structured, context-rich text prompts instead of bare class names roughly doubled zero-shot accuracy: OWLv2 improved to 13.5 mAP (+6.2 points), Grounding DINO improved to 12.4 mAP (+5.4 points) — same source (primary text fetched)
- A semi-supervised approach — Grounding DINO (Swin-B) fine-tuned using soft-ensemble pseudo-labels generated by the open-vocabulary models themselves — reached 54.3 mAP (+2.7 over the best prior closed-set result of 51.6 mAP for Co-DETR/DETA), suggesting open-vocabulary models are most useful today as a bootstrapping/pseudo-labeling tool rather than as the deployed real-time detector — same source (primary text fetched)
- By contrast, fine-tuned closed-set detectors on the same dataset reached 34.0 mAP (YOLO11-L), 35.1 mAP (RT-DETR-L), 48.3 mAP (DINO Swin-L), and 51.6 mAP (Co-DETR/DETA Swin-L) — same source (primary text fetched)
- Material-specific failure analysis (same paper): metal detection is the weakest class across all models tested, with over 50% misclassification, attributed to deformation and reflective surfaces; soft plastic, rigid plastic, and cardboard are frequently confused with each other due to flexible/translucent/visually similar appearance, with YOLO-World specifically misclassifying 65% of soft-plastic instances as cardboard; and — directly relevant to a real belt-camera deployment — background regions containing paper and **conveyor-belt textures** resembling cardboard are mislabeled as cardboard at a 60–83% rate by both OWLv2 and YOLO-World — same source (primary text fetched)
- The ZeroWaste-f dataset used for these comparisons contains 4,503 labeled images and 27,000 annotated instances across 4 material categories, with severe class imbalance (cardboard >66% of annotations, metal <2%) — same source (primary text fetched)
- No FPS or latency figures were reported for any of the open-vocabulary models (YOLO-World, Grounding DINO, OWLv2) in this paper; only training hardware (single A100 40GB GPU) was specified — same source (primary text fetched)
- General-purpose framing (not waste-specific) confirms the intended use case: open-vocabulary detectors take a free-form text prompt and return boxes for categories never seen during training, which is presented in the literature as valuable for detecting unanticipated/novel object types (example given: "abandoned suitcase," "spilled liquid on the floor") without retraining — [Open-Vocabulary Detection overview (Forasoft)](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/ai-for-video-engineering/articles-ai/open-vocabulary-detection-grounding-dino-florence-2-rtdetr-rfdetr) (search-summary only)

### Inferences
- The finding that OVOD models specifically confuse conveyor-belt background texture with cardboard (60–83% mislabel rate) is directly actionable for the pipeline being designed: if any open-vocabulary component is used (e.g., for occasional novel-material flagging), belt background/texture should be explicitly handled (masked, cropped, or included as a negative prompt class) to avoid false positives feeding the actuator.
- Given the large accuracy gap (roughly 3–7x lower mAP) versus fine-tuned closed-set detectors, and the total absence of published real-time latency figures for these open-vocabulary models on waste data, none of YOLO-World/Grounding DINO/OWLv2 currently appear justifiable as the primary in-line, actuator-triggering detector for a real-time belt-sorting pipeline; their evidenced role is closed-set-model bootstrapping (pseudo-labeling for novel classes offline) or a secondary/fallback path for flagging genuinely novel materials for human review, not the main real-time detection path.
- Because Grounding DINO and OWL-ViT/OWLv2 architectures are known in the broader open-vocabulary-detection literature (outside waste specifically) to be significantly heavier and slower than single-stage YOLO detectors due to their transformer text-vision fusion, it is reasonable to infer (though not confirmed for this domain, since no waste-specific latency numbers were found either way) that these models would need aggressive optimization (distillation, TensorRT export, reduced query counts) before being latency-viable for a belt actuator control loop.

### Gaps
- No FPS/ms latency figures for YOLO-World, Grounding DINO, or OWLv2 specifically on waste/recyclable detection were found anywhere in this research; this is a significant, explicit gap given the objective's emphasis on latency numbers.
- No paper was found applying open-vocabulary detection to a live/streaming conveyor-belt scenario (as opposed to static image benchmarking on ZeroWaste-f); the "handle novel materials without retraining" use case is discussed conceptually in the broader OVOD literature but not demonstrated end-to-end in a waste-sorting deployment.
- No comparison of open-vocabulary detection against closed-set detectors in domains other than ZeroWaste-f (e.g., e-waste, industrial parts sorting) was found, so it is unclear whether the ~3-7x accuracy gap observed generalizes across material types.

## Are there published latency budgets or throughput analyses (frames needed per object given belt speed and camera field of view) for real-time sorting systems?

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed paper was found that publishes a formal, end-to-end latency budget (detector + tracker + actuator) derived from belt speed and camera field of view for waste sorting specifically. Component-level numbers exist and can be assembled into a budget: practitioner vision-system guidance gives concrete frame-rate-vs-belt-speed rules of thumb, pneumatic ejector actuator response times are documented at single-digit milliseconds, and one specific waste-sorting CNN+robotic-arm system reports a verified 70 ms inference latency. Broader end-to-end latency and commercial-throughput figures were also found but could not be reliably attributed to specific papers, so they are reported as gaps rather than cited findings (see below).

### Cited Findings
- Practitioner vision-system sizing guidance: at a 12 in/s belt speed with a 4 in minimum part size, the calculated minimum frame rate is 3 fps, but the source recommends planning for 15–30 fps with 10–20% frame overlap to avoid parts falling between frames under variable belt speed — [Vision Sensor Selection Guide for Conveyor Part Counting (Industrial Monitor Direct)](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/selecting-a-vision-system-for-random-part-counting-on-conveyors) (search-summary only; vendor/practitioner content, not peer-reviewed, and about general part-counting rather than waste specifically)
- For a fixed camera field of view, higher belt speed requires proportionally higher frame rate to maintain detection continuity and reliability; high-speed conveyors (≥5 m/s) additionally require shorter exposure times to control motion blur, and oblique camera angles at low frame rates risk targets appearing too small/blurred for reliable detection — same source; consistent with general guidance in [Object detection on a conveyor belt (VA Imaging)](https://va-imaging.com/en-us/blogs/machine-vision-solutions-applications/object-detection-on-a-conveyor-belt-how-to-use-computer-vision) (both search-summary only, vendor content)
- Downstream actuator latency (pneumatic air-jet ejectors, the standard rejection mechanism in optical/vision sorting): current-generation pneumatic air valves take approximately 6.5 ms to open and 7.5 ms to close; next-generation valves target 3.5 ms open / 4.5 ms close — [Air spring pneumatic product rejection system (US Patent 8082838)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8082838) (search-summary only; primary source is the patent document itself, not independently fetched in full)
- Because ejector actuation has inherent delay, sorting-system controllers must compute the predicted time a given item will reach the ejection point and issue the actuation command early enough to compensate for the valve's response-time delay — same patent source (search-summary only). Pneumatic "sniper" ejectors used in color-sorting machinery are described as operating at "extremely rapid speeds, often in the range of milliseconds," specifically to keep up with high-throughput processing — [Pneumatic Sniper Ejectors in Color Sorting (Cosmos)](https://cosmos-color-sorter.com/pneumatic-sniper-ejectors-in-color-sorting-machines/) (search-summary only; vendor content)
- A CNN-based waste classification + robotic-arm system reports a mean inference latency of 70 ms at 94.5% classification accuracy and a 95.5% robotic sorting success rate — [Real-Time Automated Waste Sorting System Using CNN-Based Classification and Robotic Arm Integration (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-23547-3_13) (search-summary only, but confirmed via a dedicated follow-up search that isolated this figure specifically to this paper, giving it higher confidence than the other latency figures found)

### Inferences
- Stitching the component numbers together suggests a rough latency budget for an actuator-driving belt pipeline: camera exposure/frame acquisition (frame-rate-dependent, ideally ≥15–30 fps per the sizing guidance) + detector/tracker inference (10–40 ms achievable with nano-scale YOLO variants per Key Question 1) + decision/trajectory-prediction logic (unquantified in sources found) + actuator response (roughly 4–8 ms for pneumatic valves) must together stay below the transit time from the camera's field of view to the ejection/pick point. No source found actually performs or publishes this end-to-end calculation for a real system, so this is the research team's own inference from assembling separate component figures, not a cited result, and it should be presented as such (a proposed budget to validate experimentally, not a documented one).
- The one verified waste-specific end-to-end latency figure (70 ms, CNN classification + robotic arm) is well within the sub-100ms range needed for many belt speeds per the frame-rate sizing guidance above, suggesting a nano-YOLO-class detector plus a lightweight tracker is very likely to fit within a real-time budget on GPU-class edge hardware — but this is an inference bridging two separately-sourced figures, not a single confirmed measurement of a full detect+track+actuate loop.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source was found that explicitly derives "frames needed per object" from belt speed and field-of-view as a formal analysis for a waste- or recyclables-sorting system; the closest available guidance (Industrial Monitor Direct) is vendor/practitioner content, not an academic publication, and is for general conveyor part-counting rather than waste sorting specifically.
- Several additional latency and throughput figures surfaced during research but are **explicitly excluded from Cited Findings** because they emerged as an unattributed prose synthesis mixing several source papers, and this pass could not confirm which specific paper each number belongs to: (a) a collaborative-robot vision-sorting system said to have "2–4 second" inference/cycle time; (b) a Raspberry-Pi-based system said to have 1000–1600 ms model inference latency plus 160–220 ms preprocessing latency, yielding ~0.5–0.8 FPS effective throughput; (c) commercial/industrial throughput claims of ~7 objects/min (basic RGB system, 89% accuracy), up to 80 picks/min (AMP Robotics/ZenRobotics-class robotic sorters), 120 picks/min (an integrated materials-recovery-facility system recovering 7 material types), 200 items/min (AMP Robotics Cortex), 700 items/min (a system referred to as "Antfarm X1" in Amsterdam), and "thousands of metal items per minute" for non-robotic optical/ejector sorters. - No source quantifies tracker-added latency overhead (i.e., how much additional per-frame time ByteTrack/BoT-SORT adds on top of raw detector inference) in any belt or industrial context, which is a direct input needed for a full pipeline latency budget.
